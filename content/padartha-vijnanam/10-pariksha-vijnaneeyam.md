# Chapter 10 — Pariksha Vijnaneeyam

## NCISM First Professional BAMS — Padartha Vijnanam

### Chapter scope

**Pariksha** means examination, investigation, assessment or method of determining the nature of an object or condition. In Padartha Vijnanam, Pariksha is studied together with **Prama, Aprama, Prameya, Pramata and Pramana**, and with the methods by which valid knowledge is obtained.

The official NCISM AyUG-PV curriculum places this topic under Paper II and requires students to understand the definition, significance and necessity of Pariksha; the definitions of Prama, Aprama, Prameya, Pramata and Pramana; the enumeration of Pramanas according to philosophical schools; the Ayurvedic **Chaturvidha-Parikshavidhi**; the subsumption of Pramanas under three Pariksha; and practical application in Nidana and Chikitsa.

---

# 1. Learning objectives

After completing this chapter, the learner should be able to:

- define Pariksha;
- explain its significance, necessity and utility;
- define Prama, Aprama, Prameya, Pramata and Pramana;
- distinguish the knower, means of knowledge, object of knowledge and valid knowledge;
- explain why valid examination is essential in Ayurveda;
- describe the enumeration of Pramanas in major philosophical traditions;
- explain the Ayurvedic fourfold examination method;
- understand the relationship between Pariksha and Pramana;
- explain the grouping of Pramanas under the three Pariksha;
- apply examination methods in Nidana;
- apply examination methods in Chikitsa;
- identify common errors in clinical assessment;
- integrate classical epistemology with responsible contemporary clinical reasoning.

---

# 2. Meaning of Pariksha

The word **Pariksha (परीक्षा)** conveys the idea of examining, testing, investigating or carefully determining something.

Pariksha is not merely looking at a patient.

It is a systematic process of obtaining and evaluating information so that a sound conclusion can be reached.

### Simple clinical chain

**Question → Examination → Evidence → Interpretation → Decision**

Thus Pariksha is closely related to the Ayurvedic physician's ability to:

- identify disease;
- assess the patient;
- determine prognosis;
- select treatment;
- monitor response.

---

# 3. Definition and significance

Pariksha is the process by which an object, person, condition or proposition is examined to determine its nature.

In Ayurveda, examination is important because treatment should not be based on unsupported assumptions.

A physician must determine:

- what is present;
- what is absent;
- what is causative;
- what is pathological;
- what is physiological;
- what is the strength of the patient;
- what is the strength of disease;
- what treatment is appropriate.

---

# 4. Necessity of Pariksha

A physician may have extensive theoretical knowledge, but clinical decisions require examination.

### Why examination is necessary

1. To identify the actual condition.
2. To distinguish similar presentations.
3. To determine the cause.
4. To assess disease severity.
5. To assess patient strength.
6. To determine prognosis.
7. To select appropriate treatment.
8. To monitor treatment response.
9. To avoid contraindicated interventions.
10. To reduce diagnostic error.

### Fundamental principle

**Knowledge without appropriate examination may lead to inappropriate application.**

---

# 5. Utility of Pariksha

Pariksha has applications in:

- **Swasthavritta** — assessment of health;
- **Nidana** — diagnosis and disease assessment;
- **Chikitsa** — therapeutic decision-making;
- **Rasayana** — assessment of suitability and response;
- **research** — systematic observation and evaluation;
- **teaching** — developing clinical reasoning.

---

# 6. The five epistemological terms

A student must clearly distinguish:

1. **Prama**
2. **Aprama**
3. **Prameya**
4. **Pramata**
5. **Pramana**

These concepts form the basic framework of epistemology.

---

# 7. Prama

**Prama** means valid, true or accurate cognition/knowledge.

It is knowledge that correctly apprehends its object.

### Example

A physician correctly identifies a measurable clinical finding after appropriate examination.

### Key idea

**Prama = valid knowledge**

---

# 8. Aprama

**Aprama** is invalid, erroneous or non-valid cognition.

It may arise from:

- defective sensory conditions;
- inadequate examination;
- faulty reasoning;
- incorrect interpretation;
- misleading information;
- memory or perceptual error.

### Example

Mistaking a rope for a snake under poor visual conditions is a classical type of perceptual error.

### Clinical analogy

Calling a disease solely on the basis of one misleading observation can result in Aprama.

---

# 9. Prameya

**Prameya** is the object of valid knowledge.

Anything that is to be known or examined can be considered the object of cognition in the relevant epistemological context.

### Clinical examples

- Dosha;
- Dhatu;
- Mala;
- Agni;
- Prakriti;
- disease;
- signs and symptoms;
- patient strength;
- causative factors.

### Formula

**Prameya = that which is to be known**

---

# 10. Pramata

**Pramata** is the knower or subject who obtains valid knowledge.

In clinical practice, the physician functions as the knower when examining the patient.

However, Pramata is not simply “the doctor.”

It refers to the subject possessing the capacity to know in the epistemological framework.

### Clinical requirements of a good Pramata

- adequate knowledge;
- sound sensory function;
- attention;
- proper reasoning;
- freedom from avoidable bias;
- appropriate examination skills.

---

# 11. Pramana

**Pramana** is the means or instrument of valid knowledge.

It answers:

**How was this knowledge obtained?**

Examples include:

- perception;
- inference;
- verbal testimony;
- comparison;
- postulation and other methods depending on the philosophical school.

The exact enumeration varies among Darshanas.

---

# 12. The five terms — master table

| Term | Meaning | Clinical analogy |
|---|---|---|
| Pramata | Knower | Physician/examiner |
| Pramana | Means of valid knowledge | Examination method |
| Prameya | Object known | Patient/condition/finding |
| Prama | Valid knowledge | Correct conclusion |
| Aprama | Invalid knowledge | Incorrect conclusion |

### Memory

**Pramata knows**

**Pramana helps know**

**Prameya is known**

**Prama is valid knowledge**

**Aprama is invalid knowledge**

---

# 13. Relationship between Pramata, Pramana, Prameya and Prama

A simplified epistemic sequence is:

**Pramata**

↓ uses

**Pramana**

↓ to know

**Prameya**

↓ resulting in

**Prama**

If the process is defective, the result may become:

**Aprama**

### Clinical example

Physician  
→ uses examination  
→ examines patient  
→ obtains valid clinical knowledge.

---

# 14. Pramana and Pariksha

The two terms are closely related but should not be treated as identical.

**Pramana** is the means of valid knowledge.

**Pariksha** is examination/investigation.

An examination method may employ one or more Pramanas.

Therefore:

**Pariksha is the practical process; Pramana explains the epistemic means by which valid knowledge is obtained.**

---

# 15. Enumeration of Pramanas

Different philosophical schools accept different numbers and types of Pramana.

Commonly discussed Pramanas include:

1. **Pratyaksha** — perception
2. **Anumana** — inference
3. **Upamana** — comparison/analogy
4. **Shabda** — reliable verbal testimony
5. **Arthapatti** — postulation/presumption
6. **Anupalabdhi** — non-cognition/non-perception as a means for knowing absence

The exact enumeration and philosophical status differ among schools.

### Important examination point

Do not write that every Darshana accepts all six equally.

---

# 16. Major philosophical differences

| Darshana/tradition | Commonly associated Pramanas |
|---|---|
| Nyaya | Pratyaksha, Anumana, Upamana, Shabda |
| Vaisheshika | Traditionally emphasizes Pratyaksha and Anumana in classical formulations |
| Sankhya/Yoga | Commonly three: Pratyaksha, Anumana, Shabda |
| Mimamsa | Broader enumeration, including additional Pramanas |
| Vedanta | Often recognizes six in its developed epistemology |
| Buddhist traditions | Enumeration varies by school; commonly emphasizes Pratyaksha and Anumana in major epistemological traditions |

### Caution

The philosophical literature contains internal developments and variations. Examination answers should follow the classifications prescribed in the student's institution's reference texts.

---

# 17. Chaturvidha-Parikshavidhi

Ayurveda describes a fourfold method of examination:

1. **Aptopadesha**
2. **Pratyaksha**
3. **Anumana**
4. **Yukti**

This is called **Chaturvidha-Parikshavidhi**.

It is central to Ayurvedic clinical reasoning.

---

# 18. Aptopadesha

**Aptopadesha** is authoritative/reliable verbal instruction or testimony.

In the Ayurvedic context, it includes knowledge transmitted through reliable authoritative sources.

It is important for understanding:

- principles;
- definitions;
- classical disease descriptions;
- therapeutic procedures;
- contraindications;
- established knowledge.

### Clinical role

A physician does not rediscover every principle through direct observation.

Authoritative knowledge provides the conceptual framework within which examination and reasoning occur.

---

# 19. Pratyaksha

**Pratyaksha** is direct perception.

In practical examination it includes appropriate use of the senses and direct observation.

Examples:

- observing the patient's appearance;
- hearing speech;
- observing gait;
- palpating appropriate physical findings;
- examining visible signs.

### Important limitation

Not everything can be known directly through perception.

Therefore Pratyaksha must be combined with other methods.

---

# 20. Anumana

**Anumana** is inference.

It allows knowledge of something not directly perceived through an appropriate logical relation.

### Simple example

Smoke is observed.

Fire is inferred because smoke is known to be associated with fire under the relevant conditions.

### Clinical application

A physician may infer:

- Dosha involvement;
- disease stage;
- prognosis;
- underlying pathology;

from a combination of observed signs and established relations.

---

# 21. Yukti

**Yukti** is rational, systematic and integrative reasoning based on the coordinated consideration of multiple factors.

It is particularly important in Ayurveda because clinical decisions often involve several interacting variables.

### Examples

Considering together:

- Dosha;
- Dushya;
- Agni;
- Bala;
- Kala;
- Desha;
- Ahara;
- Vihara;
- age;
- disease stage;
- treatment strength.

### Clinical principle

**Yukti integrates multiple observations into a practical decision.**

---

# 22. Fourfold examination — master table

| Pariksha | Core idea | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Aptopadesha | Reliable authoritative instruction | Classical description |
| Pratyaksha | Direct perception | Observed sign |
| Anumana | Inference | Inferring hidden condition |
| Yukti | Rational integration | Selecting treatment from multiple factors |

---

# 23. Subsumption of Pramanas under three Pariksha

NCISM specifically requires understanding the subsumption of different Pramanas under **three Pariksha**.

A commonly taught Ayurvedic framework uses:

1. Aptopadesha
2. Pratyaksha
3. Anumana

with **Yukti** treated as an important additional method of reasoning in the Chaturvidha-Parikshavidhi.

The exact mapping can vary with the explanatory tradition and prescribed textbook.

### Examination caution

Write the classification exactly as taught in the institution's prescribed text when a question specifically asks for the threefold versus fourfold Pariksha.

---

# 24. Pariksha in Nidana

Diagnosis requires systematic examination.

### Step 1 — Gather reliable information

Use:

- patient history;
- authoritative disease knowledge;
- direct findings.

### Step 2 — Observe

Use Pratyaksha.

### Step 3 — Infer

Use Anumana.

### Step 4 — Integrate

Use Yukti.

### Step 5 — Arrive at a conclusion

Determine:

- disease;
- Dosha;
- Dushya;
- Nidana;
- stage;
- severity;
- prognosis.

---

# 25. Pariksha in Chikitsa

Treatment selection requires more than identifying a disease name.

The physician should examine:

- patient strength;
- disease strength;
- Agni;
- age;
- season;
- habitat;
- tolerance;
- associated conditions;
- previous treatment;
- contraindications.

Then treatment is selected using integrated reasoning.

### Formula

**Examination → Assessment → Treatment selection → Monitoring → Re-examination**

---

# 26. Pariksha and patient assessment

A useful Ayurvedic clinical assessment separates:

### Patient factors

- Prakriti;
- Vikriti;
- Satva;
- Satmya;
- Ahara;
- Bala;
- Vaya;
- Kala;
- Desha.

### Disease factors

- Nidana;
- Dosha;
- Dushya;
- Srotas;
- Sthana;
- Avastha;
- severity;
- prognosis.

The exact examination framework depends on the clinical context and prescribed Ayurvedic methodology.

---

# 27. Pariksha and diagnostic error

Errors may arise when:

- history is incomplete;
- the examiner is inattentive;
- sensory information is unreliable;
- an inference is made without adequate basis;
- contradictory evidence is ignored;
- an authority is followed without contextual application;
- multiple factors are not integrated.

### Epistemological lesson

A correct conclusion requires both:

**appropriate evidence + appropriate reasoning.**

---

# 28. Pariksha and bias

A clinician can make errors because of cognitive bias.

Examples include:

- premature closure;
- confirmation bias;
- anchoring;
- availability bias.

These modern terms can help students understand why examination must remain systematic.

They are explanatory analogies and should not be presented as direct Sanskrit-equivalents of classical epistemological categories.

---

# 29. Pratyaksha — basic conceptual analysis

Pratyaksha is commonly described through direct perception.

A basic sequence is:

**Sense organ → sense object → appropriate contact/condition → cognition**

The philosophical literature provides detailed analyses of perceptual cognition.

For examination purposes, the student should understand that perception requires appropriate conditions and can be affected by defects.

---

# 30. Limitations of direct perception

Direct perception may be affected by:

- excessive distance;
- excessive proximity;
- poor illumination;
- obstruction;
- defective sensory organs;
- distraction;
- similarity/confusion;
- rapid movement;
- other interfering conditions.

Therefore:

**Not perceived ≠ necessarily absent.**

This is especially important in clinical practice.

---

# 31. Anumana in clinical reasoning

Inference is especially useful when the target is not directly observable.

### Clinical pattern

Observed signs  
→ established relation  
→ inference  
→ provisional conclusion  
→ confirmation/refinement through further examination.

### Example

A set of signs may lead a physician to infer Dosha predominance.

The inference should remain proportionate to the evidence.

---

# 32. Yukti and multi-factor reasoning

Ayurvedic clinical reasoning frequently requires simultaneous consideration of several variables.

For example, the same intervention may have different effects depending on:

- Dosha;
- Agni;
- Bala;
- Kala;
- Desha;
- age;
- disease stage.

Yukti helps integrate these variables rather than treating them as isolated facts.

---

# 33. Contemporary scientific comparison

The fourfold examination method can be compared conceptually with modern evidence-based clinical reasoning:

| Ayurvedic method | Contemporary analogy |
|---|---|
| Aptopadesha | Established literature/guidelines |
| Pratyaksha | Direct observation/examination |
| Anumana | Inference/diagnostic reasoning |
| Yukti | Integrated clinical reasoning |

### Important limitation

These are **functional analogies**, not statements that classical Ayurvedic epistemology and modern evidence-based medicine are identical systems.

---

# 34. Pariksha and research

The epistemological framework is useful for research.

A research process may involve:

1. defining the question;
2. identifying what is to be known;
3. selecting appropriate methods;
4. collecting observations;
5. analyzing data;
6. drawing justified conclusions;
7. checking alternative explanations.

This parallels the general logic of disciplined examination.

# 38. Common misconceptions

### Misconception 1
**Pramana and Pariksha are identical.**

**Correction:** Pramana is the means of valid knowledge; Pariksha is examination/investigation.

### Misconception 2
**Pratyaksha means only seeing.**

**Correction:** Direct perception involves appropriate sensory cognition, not merely visual observation.

### Misconception 3
**Inference is guessing.**

**Correction:** Valid Anumana depends on an appropriate logical relation and evidence.

### Misconception 4
**Yukti means intuition.**

**Correction:** Yukti is systematic, rational integration of multiple factors.

### Misconception 5
**Every philosophical school accepts the same number of Pramanas.**

**Correction:** Enumeration differs among Darshanas.

### Misconception 6
**A negative examination proves complete absence of disease.**

**Correction:** Failure to detect a finding can have multiple explanations.

# 49. Summary

Pariksha is the systematic examination of a person, object, condition or proposition to obtain reliable knowledge. It is essential in Ayurveda because diagnosis and treatment must be based on appropriate examination rather than unsupported assumptions.

The central epistemological terms are:

- **Pramata** — knower;
- **Pramana** — means of valid knowledge;
- **Prameya** — object of knowledge;
- **Prama** — valid knowledge;
- **Aprama** — invalid knowledge.

Different Darshanas enumerate Pramanas differently. Important Pramanas include Pratyaksha, Anumana, Upamana, Shabda, Arthapatti and Anupalabdhi, depending on the philosophical system.

Ayurveda teaches **Chaturvidha-Parikshavidhi**:

1. Aptopadesha
2. Pratyaksha
3. Anumana
4. Yukti

These methods support examination in Nidana and Chikitsa. Direct observation, inference and integrated reasoning must work together, while authoritative knowledge provides the conceptual foundation.

The major practical lesson is:

**Good clinical knowledge requires a suitable object of examination, an appropriate method of knowing, competent examination, careful interpretation and rational integration.**
