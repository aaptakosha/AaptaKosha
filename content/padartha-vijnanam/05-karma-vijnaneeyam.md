# 2. Meaning and etymology of Karma

**Karma (कर्म)** is derived from the Sanskrit verbal root **√kṛ — to do, act or perform**.

In ordinary language, Karma means action or activity. In Padartha Vijnanam, however, it is a technical category with a specific philosophical meaning.

A useful introductory definition is:

> **Karma is activity/action that resides in Dravya and is responsible for movement or change of position.**

In the Vaisheshika framework, Karma is one of the Padarthas and is distinct from Guna.

### Core relationship

**Dravya → Ashraya**  
**Guna → quality residing in Dravya**  
**Karma → action/activity of Dravya**

Conceptual diagram:

**Dravya**  
↓ possesses  
**Guna**  
↓ and performs  
**Karma**  
↓ producing  
**conjunction / disjunction / movement-related effect**

---

# 3. Karma as a Padartha

The classical six Padarthas of Vaisheshika are:

1. Dravya
2. Guna
3. Karma
4. Samanya
5. Vishesha
6. Samavaya

Later Nyaya-Vaisheshika discussions add **Abhava**, making seven categories in the expanded framework.

Karma is therefore a fundamental category for understanding activity.

### Why Karma is different from Guna

| Guna | Karma |
|---|---|
| Quality/property | Action/activity |
| Resides in Dravya | Resides in Dravya |
| Does not itself produce conjunction/disjunction as Karma | Produces conjunction/disjunction |
| Example: Guru, Snigdha | Example: movement |

Both depend on Dravya, but they are not interchangeable.

---

# 4. Karma Lakshana according to Darshana

Vaisheshika defines Karma as a category that resides in Dravya and is the immediate cause of conjunction and disjunction.

Important features:

- Karma is a property of Dravya in the sense of a distinct Padartha.
- It is dynamic rather than static.
- It produces conjunction and disjunction.
- It is momentary/transitory in the classical framework.
- It is not the substratum of another Karma.
- A single Dravya may possess Karma at a given time.
- Karma is closely connected with movement.

### Key exam phrase

**Karma is the cause of conjunction and disjunction.**

This is one of the most important distinctions between Karma and Guna.

---

# 5. Karma according to Ayurveda

Ayurveda uses Karma in a broader and highly practical sense.

Examples include:

- Dosha Karma,
- Dhatu Karma,
- Mala Karma,
- Agni Karma,
- Aushadha Karma,
- therapeutic Karma,
- physiological action,
- procedural action.

Thus, Ayurvedic use of Karma often refers to the **function, action or effect produced by a substance, tissue, Dosha, therapy or process**.

### Important caution

Do not automatically assume that every Ayurvedic use of the word Karma is identical to the narrow Vaisheshika Padartha definition.

**Context determines meaning.**

---

# 6. Karma Lakshana — Darshana versus Charaka/Ayurveda

The philosophical and Ayurvedic approaches overlap but have different emphases.

| Point | Darshana/Vaisheshika | Ayurveda/Charaka |
|---|---|---|
| Central meaning | Activity/movement | Function/action/effect in context |
| Main philosophical role | Padartha | Practical/clinical reasoning |
| Relation to Dravya | Resides in Dravya | Action of a substance/system |
| Conjunction/disjunction | Central | Not the sole focus |
| Therapeutic application | Indirect | Extensive |
| Example | Utksepana | Aushadha Karma |

### Examination rule

When asked **“Karma according to Vaisheshika”**, emphasize the five classical movement categories.

When asked **“Karma according to Ayurveda”**, explain function/action and then give appropriate Ayurvedic examples.

---

# 7. Classification of Karma — five Laukika Karmas

Vaisheshika classically recognizes five types of physical/movement Karma:

1. **Utksepana** — upward movement
2. **Avaksepana** — downward movement
3. **Akunchana** — contraction
4. **Prasarana** — expansion/extension
5. **Gamana** — locomotion/general movement

### Memory formula

**U-A-A-P-G**

- Utksepana
- Avaksepana
- Akunchana
- Prasarana
- Gamana

---

# 8. Utksepana

**Utksepana** means upward movement.

Examples:
- throwing an object upward,
- raising the arm,
- lifting a weight.

Conceptual diagram:

**Object/body part → ↑**

The important characteristic is movement in an upward direction.

### Practical examples

- lifting a cup,
- raising the hand,
- jumping upward.

---

# 9. Avaksepana

**Avaksepana** means downward movement.

Examples:
- dropping an object,
- lowering the arm,
- movement downward under gravity.

Conceptual diagram:

**Object/body part → ↓**

It is the directional counterpart of Utksepana.

---

# 10. Akunchana

**Akunchana** means contraction or bending inward.

Examples:
- flexion of a limb,
- drawing a structure inward,
- contraction of a muscular structure.

Conceptual diagram:

**Extended structure → inward/bent position**

It is especially useful as a conceptual category for contraction or reduction of extension.

---

# 11. Prasarana

**Prasarana** means expansion, extension or spreading outward.

Examples:
- extending the arm,
- straightening a flexed joint,
- opening/spreading a structure.

Conceptual diagram:

**Flexed/contracted structure → extended position**

### Akunchana versus Prasarana

| Akunchana | Prasarana |
|---|---|
| Contraction/flexion | Extension/expansion |
| Inward | Outward |
| Reduces extension | Increases extension |

---

# 12. Gamana

**Gamana** is movement from one place to another and includes locomotion or general movement.

Examples:
- walking,
- running,
- movement of an object from one location to another.

Conceptual diagram:

**Place A → movement → Place B**

Gamana is broader than merely upward/downward movement.

---

# 13. Five Karmas — master table

| Karma | Meaning | Simple example |
|---|---|---|
| Utksepana | Upward movement | Raising arm |
| Avaksepana | Downward movement | Lowering arm |
| Akunchana | Contraction/flexion | Bending limb |
| Prasarana | Extension/expansion | Straightening limb |
| Gamana | General locomotion | Walking |

### Rapid memory

**Utksepana = up**  
**Avaksepana = down**  
**Akunchana = contract**  
**Prasarana = extend**  
**Gamana = move**

---

# 14. Laukika Karma

**Laukika Karma** refers to ordinary/worldly physical activity and movement considered within the classical philosophical framework.

The five categories above describe Laukika movement.

A classical example can be understood through an object moving from one location to another:

**Dravya at position A → Karma → conjunction/disjunction → new position B**

This is different from an Ayurvedic therapeutic Karma such as the action of a medicine.

---

# 15. Production of Laukika Karma

Karma arises in Dravya under appropriate causal conditions.

A simplified teaching sequence is:

**Dravya + causal conditions → Karma → movement → new conjunction/disjunction**

The exact causal account varies according to the philosophical discussion.

### Example: lifting a ball

1. Ball is a Dravya.
2. A force/action is applied.
3. Upward movement occurs.
4. The ball changes its spatial relation.
5. Utksepana is recognized as the relevant Karma.

---

# 16. Destruction of Laukika Karma

Karma is not permanent. Once the particular movement ceases, that Karma is destroyed.

Example:

**Ball stationary → upward movement begins → Utksepana → movement stops → that Karma ceases**

This is important because Karma is a transient category.

### Production–destruction diagram

**No movement**  
↓ causal condition  
**Karma produced**  
↓  
**Movement**  
↓ movement ceases  
**Karma destroyed**

---

# 17. Karma and conjunction/disjunction

One of the most important philosophical concepts is the relationship between Karma and **Samyoga/Vibhaga**.

### Samyoga

Conjunction or coming together.

### Vibhaga

Disjunction or separation.

Karma produces changes in spatial relationships.

Example:

**Hand + object → Samyoga**

If the hand moves away:

**Hand ← movement → object → Vibhaga**

Thus:

**Karma → change in relation → Samyoga/Vibhaga**

---

# 18. Karma and Dravya

Karma cannot exist independently of Dravya.

A useful model is:

**Dravya = actor/substratum**  
**Karma = activity of that Dravya**

Example:
- A hand is a material Dravya.
- Raising the hand is Karma.

Do not describe Karma as an independent physical substance.

---

# 19. Karma and Guna

Karma and Guna are both associated with Dravya but differ fundamentally.

### Example

A ball:
- spherical form → Guna/property,
- upward movement → Karma.

Therefore:

**Form = Guna**  
**Movement = Karma**

This simple example is useful for viva examinations.

---

# 20. Karma in Ayurveda — functional application

Ayurveda frequently uses Karma to describe what a Dravya, Dosha, Dhatu, Mala or therapy does.

Examples:

- **Vamana Karma** — therapeutic emesis procedure.
- **Virechana Karma** — therapeutic purgation procedure.
- **Basti Karma** — therapeutic enema-based procedure.
- **Rasayana Karma** — rejuvenative action.
- **Brimhana Karma** — nourishing/building action.
- **Langhana Karma** — lightening/reducing action.
- **Deepana Karma** — promoting digestive/metabolic capacity.
- **Pachana Karma** — supporting digestion/metabolism of Ama-related material in the classical framework.

These are Ayurvedic functional/therapeutic uses and should not be confused with the five Vaisheshika Karmas.

---

# 21. Karma of Dosha

Each Dosha has characteristic functions.

### Vata Karma

Vata is associated with:
- movement,
- propulsion,
- sensory and motor activity,
- circulation of physiological processes,
- elimination and other movement-dependent functions.

### Pitta Karma

Pitta is associated with:
- transformation,
- digestion,
- metabolism,
- heat,
- visual and discriminative functions in the relevant Ayurvedic framework.

### Kapha Karma

Kapha is associated with:
- stability,
- lubrication,
- cohesion,
- support,
- strength and nourishment-related functions.

The word Karma here means **functional activity**, not one of the five Vaisheshika movement categories.

---

# 22. Karma of Dhatu

Ayurveda describes functional roles of the seven Dhatus.

Examples of functional reasoning:

| Dhatu | Broad functional role |
|---|---|
| Rasa | Nourishment and fluid transport |
| Rakta | Life-supporting circulation and vitality |
| Mamsa | Covering and structural support |
| Meda | Lubrication and energy/storage-related function |
| Asthi | Support |
| Majja | Filling/nourishment within bony structures and nervous-system associations in Ayurvedic interpretation |
| Shukra | Reproductive and generative function |

These are broad educational summaries. The classical descriptions should be studied from the prescribed Samhita and commentary.

---

# 23. Karma of Mala

Mala are also described through their functional roles.

Examples:
- Purisha supports certain structural functions and maintains normal bowel function.
- Mutra participates in fluid/waste elimination.
- Sveda contributes to regulation and excretion through sweating.

The exact classical functions should be memorized from the prescribed text.

---

# 24. Aushadha Karma

A medicinal substance may be described according to its Karma.

Examples include:
- Deepana,
- Pachana,
- Anulomana,
- Rechana,
- Vamana,
- Grahi,
- Mutrala,
- Swedajanana,
- Balya,
- Brimhana,
- Langhana.

### Important principle

Aushadha Karma is not determined by a single property.

A better model is:

**Dravya + Rasa + Guna + Virya + Vipaka + Prabhava + Matra + patient factors → Karma**

---

# 25. Therapeutic Karma and action-selection

Clinical reasoning can be represented as:

**Patient assessment**  
↓  
**Dosha/Vikriti + Agni + Bala + Kala + Desha**  
↓  
**Identify desired therapeutic action**  
↓  
**Select appropriate Dravya/procedure**  
↓  
**Evaluate response**

Example:

If the therapeutic objective is to reduce excessive heaviness, an appropriate **Langhana** direction may be considered, but the actual selection depends on the patient's full condition.

---

# 26. Karma and Panchakarma

The word Karma appears prominently in procedural Ayurveda.

Examples:
- Vamana,
- Virechana,
- Basti,
- Nasya,
- Raktamokshana in relevant procedural contexts.

These are **therapeutic procedures/actions**, not the five Vaisheshika Karmas.

### Examination trap

Question: “Name the five Karmas according to Vaisheshika.”

**Correct:** Utksepana, Avaksepana, Akunchana, Prasarana, Gamana.

Do **not** answer Vamana, Virechana, Basti, etc.

# 28. Contemporary science and Karma

NCISM specifically asks for contemporary-science principles/examples that enhance understanding of Karma.

These examples are **analogies**, not proof that classical Karma and modern mechanics are identical.

| Classical Karma | Useful contemporary analogy | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Utksepana | upward displacement/velocity | classical category is not a physics equation |
| Avaksepana | downward displacement | not identical to gravitational acceleration |
| Akunchana | muscular contraction/flexion | classical meaning is broader |
| Prasarana | extension/expansion | not identical to all forms of expansion |
| Gamana | locomotion/displacement | classical category is conceptual |
| Samyoga | contact/coming together | not equivalent to molecular bonding |
| Vibhaga | separation | broader than physical separation alone |

### Important exam statement

> Contemporary mechanics can help visualize movement, displacement and interaction, but the Vaisheshika category of Karma should not be reduced to modern kinematics.

---

# 29. Karma and movement in the human body

The human body contains numerous movements:
- flexion,
- extension,
- locomotion,
- respiration,
- circulation,
- peristalsis,
- muscular contraction.

Some can serve as modern analogies for understanding movement categories, but Ayurvedic Karma has its own theoretical context.

### Integrated model

**Vata → movement principle**  
↓  
**Musculoskeletal + physiological activity**  
↓  
**Observable Karma**

This does not mean Vata is identical to a modern physical force.

---

# 30. Karma and Vata

Vata has a particularly important relationship with movement.

Traditional Ayurvedic reasoning associates Vata with:
- gati,
- pravritti,
- movement of physiological processes,
- initiation and coordination of activity.

Therefore, **Karma is an important bridge between Padartha theory and functional Ayurveda.**

---

# 31. Karma and Samanya–Vishesha

The later concepts of Samanya and Vishesha provide an important therapeutic reasoning framework.

- Similarity tends to increase.
- Difference tends to decrease.

Thus, the desired therapeutic Karma can be supported by selecting a Dravya whose qualities and actions are appropriate to the condition.

Example:

**Excessive heaviness → choose appropriate Laghu/Langhana direction**

This should never be reduced to a single-rule prescription.

---

# 32. Karma and Yukti

Yukti integrates multiple factors to choose an appropriate action.

Conceptual diagram:

**Disease + patient + Dravya + Guna + Kala + Desha + Matra**  
↓  
**Yukti**  
↓  
**appropriate Karma**

This is an important connection between Karma Vijnanam and practical clinical reasoning.

---

# 33. Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Correct understanding |
|---|---|
| Karma always means “fate” | In Padartha Vijnanam it primarily means action/activity |
| Karma and Guna are identical | Guna is quality; Karma is action |
| Vaisheshika has only one kind of Karma | Five classical movement categories are described |
| Vamana/Virechana are the five Vaisheshika Karmas | They are Ayurvedic therapeutic procedures |
| Gamana means only walking | It is general locomotion/movement |
| Utksepana means only throwing | It includes upward movement generally |
| Karma is permanent | Classical Karma is transient |
| Every Ayurvedic “Karma” has the same philosophical meaning | Meaning depends on context |
| Modern mechanics proves Vaisheshika Karma | Modern science provides analogies, not automatic equivalence |

# 34. High-yield examination points

- Karma is a fundamental **Padartha**.
- It resides in **Dravya**.
- Karma is associated with **movement/activity**.
- In Vaisheshika, Karma is the immediate cause of **Samyoga and Vibhaga**.
- Classical Vaisheshika recognizes **five Karmas**.
- **Utksepana = upward movement.**
- **Avaksepana = downward movement.**
- **Akunchana = contraction/flexion.**
- **Prasarana = extension/expansion.**
- **Gamana = locomotion/general movement.**
- Laukika Karma is transient.
- Ayurveda uses Karma more broadly for **function/action/effect**.
- Therapeutic Karma must not be confused with the five Vaisheshika Karmas.
- Yukti supports selection of appropriate Karma.
- Contemporary science may provide explanatory analogies but should not be treated as proof of identity.

# 39. Case-based applications

### Case 1 — physical movement

A student raises a book from the table to shoulder height.

**Answer:** Utksepana, because the movement is upward.

### Case 2 — lowering

The student returns the book to the table.

**Answer:** Avaksepana, because the movement is downward.

### Case 3 — bending

The elbow is flexed.

**Answer:** Akunchana.

### Case 4 — straightening

The elbow is extended.

**Answer:** Prasarana.

### Case 5 — walking

A person moves from one room to another.

**Answer:** Gamana.

### Case 6 — therapeutic decision

A patient presents with a condition where reducing excessive heaviness is a therapeutic objective.

**Reasoning:** A Langhana-oriented Karma may be considered, but the final choice requires assessment of Dosha, Agni, Bala, age, Kala, Desha and disease stage.

# 44. Summary

Karma is a fundamental Padartha and a key concept for understanding activity. In Vaisheshika it is a dynamic category residing in Dravya and serving as the immediate cause of conjunction and disjunction. Five classical Laukika Karmas are described: Utksepana, Avaksepana, Akunchana, Prasarana and Gamana.

Ayurveda uses the term Karma more broadly to express the function or action of Dosha, Dhatu, Mala, Aushadha and therapeutic procedures. This broader usage is essential for understanding the transition from philosophical Padartha theory to clinical Ayurvedic reasoning.

Karma is not Guna, and Ayurvedic therapeutic Karmas are not the same as the five Vaisheshika Karmas. The student must always interpret the term according to context.

Contemporary mechanics, physiology and movement science can help visualize selected aspects of Karma, but these modern descriptions should remain explanatory analogies rather than claims of exact equivalence.
