# 2. What is Padartha?

## 2.1 Word meaning

**Padartha (पदार्थ)** may be analysed as:

**Pada + Artha**

- **Pada** — word/term
- **Artha** — meaning/object signified by the word

Thus, Padartha broadly denotes **that which is signified by a word and is available for meaningful cognition/discussion**.

Padartha Vijnanam therefore studies the fundamental categories through which Ayurveda and Indian philosophical systems describe reality, knowledge and relationships.

---

# 3. Why Padartha is important in Ayurveda

A BAMS student encounters statements such as:

- Dravya has Guna and Karma.
- Similar substances increase similar factors.
- Opposite factors reduce or counteract each other.
- A cause produces an effect.
- Knowledge is obtained through valid means of cognition.

These are not isolated facts. They depend upon a conceptual vocabulary.

```text
             Ayurvedic clinical statement
                       │
                       ▼
                Underlying concept
                       │
             ┌─────────┼─────────┐
             ▼         ▼         ▼
          Dravya      Guna      Karma
             │         │         │
             └─────────┼─────────┘
                       ▼
                 Ayurvedic reasoning
                       │
                       ▼
                 Clinical application
```

Padartha Vijnanam provides the intellectual foundation for this reasoning.

# 4. Padartha in different traditions

The word **Padartha** is used across Indian philosophical traditions, but the exact enumeration and interpretation vary.

| Tradition | Important categories/framework |
|---|---|
| **Vaisheshika** | Dravya, Guna, Karma, Samanya, Vishesha, Samavaya, Abhava |
| **Nyaya** | Sixteen categories (Padarthas) concerned especially with valid reasoning and debate |
| **Sankhya** | Twenty-five Tattvas |
| **Yoga** | Closely related to Sankhya metaphysics with emphasis on practice and liberation |
| **Mimamsa** | Detailed analysis of knowledge, language, ritual and reality |
| **Vedanta** | Brahman, Atman, Jagat and liberation interpreted through Vedantic schools |
| **Ayurveda** | Uses selected philosophical categories and develops them for life, health, disease and treatment |

**Important:** These systems should not be treated as identical. Their terminology may overlap while their definitions and philosophical purposes differ.

---

# 5. Darshana — meaning

**Darshana (दर्शन)** comes from the verbal root associated with **seeing/perceiving**.

In the philosophical context, Darshana means a systematic way of understanding reality, knowledge, the self, causation and liberation.

It is therefore more than simply a “view” or “opinion”.

---

# 6. Six classical Astika Darshanas

The six commonly recognized orthodox systems are:

1. **Nyaya**
2. **Vaisheshika**
3. **Sankhya**
4. **Yoga**
5. **Purva Mimamsa**
6. **Vedanta / Uttara Mimamsa**

# 7. Astika and Nastika — important clarification

In introductory teaching, the six Darshanas above are called **Astika**.

The classification should not be reduced simply to “believes in God” versus “does not believe in God”. In the classical Indian philosophical context, the term has a more technical history and may relate to acceptance of the authority of the Veda, depending on the context of classification.

**Exam caution:** Do not write an oversimplified “Astika = theist” definition without qualification.

# 8. Nyaya Darshana

## 8.1 Central emphasis

Nyaya is particularly important for:

- logic
- valid knowledge
- inference
- debate
- examination of means of knowledge
- identification of errors in reasoning

This becomes highly relevant when Padartha Vijnanam later studies **Pramana**, **Anumana**, **Vyapti**, **Hetu** and **Hetvabhasa**.

## 8.2 Nyaya's sixteen Padarthas

The traditional sixteen categories are:

| No. | Category | Broad meaning |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Pramana | Means of valid knowledge |
| 2 | Prameya | Object of valid knowledge |
| 3 | Samsaya | Doubt |
| 4 | Prayojana | Purpose |
| 5 | Drishtanta | Example |
| 6 | Siddhanta | Established doctrine |
| 7 | Avayava | Members of an inference |
| 8 | Tarka | Reasoned hypothetical examination |
| 9 | Nirnaya | Determinate conclusion |
| 10 | Vada | Truth-oriented discussion |
| 11 | Jalpa | Debate aimed at victory |
| 12 | Vitanda | Destructive criticism without establishing one's own thesis |
| 13 | Hetvabhasa | Fallacy of reason |
| 14 | Chala | Quibble/misinterpretation |
| 15 | Jati | Sophistical objection |
| 16 | Nigrahasthana | Ground of defeat |

These categories provide the intellectual background for later study of Ayurvedic examination and reasoning.

---

# 9. Vaisheshika Darshana

## 9.1 Central emphasis

Vaisheshika develops a detailed analysis of reality through categories called **Padarthas**.

The classical seven categories are:

1. Dravya
2. Guna
3. Karma
4. Samanya
5. Vishesha
6. Samavaya
7. Abhava

The first six are often described in older presentations; **Abhava** was subsequently recognized as an additional category in the developed seven-fold classification.

---

# 10. Seven Vaisheshika Padarthas

## 10.1 Dravya

**Dravya** is the substantive category in which qualities and actions inhere.

Ayurveda later gives Dravya a central place in understanding substances, including the five Mahabhutas, Atma, Manas and other accepted Dravyas.

## 10.2 Guna

**Guna** denotes quality.

Qualities characterize a Dravya but are not themselves independent substances.

Ayurveda develops an extensive theory of Guna, including the **Gurvadi Gunas**.

## 10.3 Karma

**Karma** denotes action/motion.

Ayurveda uses Karma in a broader applied framework, including the actions of substances and therapeutic procedures.

## 10.4 Samanya

**Samanya** represents commonality or similarity.

In Ayurveda, Samanya becomes especially important in explaining increase through similarity.

## 10.5 Vishesha

**Vishesha** represents differentiation, particularity or dissimilarity.

Ayurvedic reasoning uses Vishesha to explain reduction or differentiation through opposite qualities/factors.

## 10.6 Samavaya

**Samavaya** is an inseparable relation.

It is a technical philosophical concept and should not be casually equated with every type of association.

## 10.7 Abhava

**Abhava** means absence/non-existence.

It becomes important in Ayurvedic and philosophical discussions of what is absent, unavailable or not existent.

# 12. Sankhya Darshana

## 12.1 Central emphasis

Sankhya presents reality through a systematic enumeration of **Tattvas**.

The classical framework recognizes **25 Tattvas**.

### The 25 Tattvas

| Group | Tattvas |
|---|---|
| Purusha | 1 |
| Prakriti | 1 |
| Mahat/Buddhi | 1 |
| Ahamkara | 1 |
| Tanmatras | 5 |
| Jnanendriyas | 5 |
| Karmendriyas | 5 |
| Manas | 1 |
| Mahabhutas | 5 |
| **Total** | **25** |

### Evolutionary sequence

```text
                    Prakriti
                       │
                       ▼
                     Mahat
                    /     \
                   /       \
             Ahamkara     (Buddhi already evolved)
                │
       ┌────────┼─────────┐
       │        │         │
    Sattvika   Rajas    Tamasa
       │                  │
       ▼                  ▼
Manas + Jnanendriya   Tanmatra
   + Karmendriya          │
                          ▼
                    Mahabhuta
```

Rajas is commonly described as having an activating/mediating role in the evolutionary process.

---

# 13. Purusha and Prakriti

This distinction is fundamental to Sankhya thought.

| Purusha | Prakriti |
|---|---|
| Conscious principle | Primordial material/nature principle |
| Witness/knower | Source of manifest evolution |
| Not an evolute in the same way as Prakriti | Evolves into successive Tattvas |
| Plurality of Purushas is accepted in classical Sankhya | One Prakriti is described in the classical framework |

**Ayurvedic relevance:** Ayurvedic concepts of Purusha, Atma, Manas, Prakriti and Mahabhuta should be studied comparatively rather than assuming that every Ayurvedic use is philosophically identical to Sankhya usage.

---

# 14. Triguna

Prakriti is described through three Gunas:

1. **Sattva**
2. **Rajas**
3. **Tamas**

| Guna | Broad characteristic |
|---|---|
| Sattva | Clarity, lightness, illumination |
| Rajas | Activity, movement, stimulation |
| Tamas | Inertia, heaviness, obscuration |

### Balance model

```text
                 PRAKRITI
                    │
          ┌─────────┼─────────┐
          ▼         ▼         ▼
       SATTVA      RAJAS     TAMAS
       clarity    activity   inertia
          │         │         │
          └─────────┼─────────┘
                    ▼
               Evolution
```

The Triguna concept is especially relevant later while studying **Manas-Prakriti** and the mental qualities in Ayurveda.

---

# 15. Yoga Darshana

Yoga is closely related to Sankhya metaphysics but places major emphasis on:

- discipline
- mental control
- cessation of mental modifications
- meditation
- ethical practice
- liberation

The **Ashtanga Yoga** framework is:

1. Yama
2. Niyama
3. Asana
4. Pranayama
5. Pratyahara
6. Dharana
7. Dhyana
8. Samadhi

### Eight-limbed path

```text
Yama
  ↓
Niyama
  ↓
Asana
  ↓
Pranayama
  ↓
Pratyahara
  ↓
Dharana
  ↓
Dhyana
  ↓
Samadhi
```

**Ayurvedic relevance:** Yoga contributes to understanding mental discipline, preventive health and the management of lifestyle-related disturbances, but Yoga Darshana and Ayurveda should not be treated as interchangeable disciplines.

---

# 16. Purva Mimamsa

Purva Mimamsa emphasizes:

- Vedic authority
- Dharma
- interpretation of Vedic injunctions
- language and meaning
- valid knowledge

Its detailed theories of **Shabda** and textual interpretation become useful when studying authoritative verbal testimony.

---

# 17. Vedanta

Vedanta, or Uttara Mimamsa, is primarily associated with:

- Brahman
- Atman
- relationship between self and ultimate reality
- liberation

Vedanta contains multiple sub-schools, including Advaita, Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita, which should not be treated as a single identical doctrine.

# 20. Charaka's Karana-Padartha

In the Charaka tradition, six categories are particularly emphasized as **Karana-Padarthas**:

1. **Samanya**
2. **Vishesha**
3. **Dravya**
4. **Guna**
5. **Karma**
6. **Samavaya**

These categories are highly important for understanding Ayurvedic causation and therapeutic reasoning.

### Six Karana-Padarthas

| Padartha | Broad role in Ayurvedic reasoning |
|---|---|
| Samanya | Similarity/commonality |
| Vishesha | Difference/opposition |
| Dravya | Substance |
| Guna | Quality |
| Karma | Action |
| Samavaya | Inseparable relation |

**High-yield point:** The Charaka-oriented six-category framework and the developed Vaisheshika seven-category framework are related but should not be presented as numerically identical.

---

# 21. Samanya and Vishesha — clinical importance

Samanya and Vishesha form a particularly important pair in Ayurveda.

### Simplified principle

**Samanya → tendency toward increase**

**Vishesha → tendency toward decrease**

Example:

| Situation | Principle |
|---|---|
| Similar qualities/factors are introduced | Samanya |
| Opposite qualities/factors are introduced | Vishesha |

This principle later becomes clinically important in understanding diet, drug action and management strategies.

# 23. Bhava and Abhava

## 23.1 Bhava

**Bhava** broadly denotes existence/being or positive presence.

In philosophical discussion it may refer to what is existent or positively established.

## 23.2 Abhava

**Abhava** denotes absence/non-existence.

It is important to understand Abhava as a technical philosophical category rather than simply translating it as “nothing”.

### Conceptual distinction

| Bhava | Abhava |
|---|---|
| Presence/existence | Absence/non-existence |
| Positive entity/state | Absence of the relevant entity/state |
| Studied as an existent category | Studied as an absence category |

---

# 24. Types of Abhava

The developed Nyaya-Vaisheshika tradition commonly discusses four forms:

1. **Pragabhava** — prior absence
2. **Pradhvamsabhava** — posterior absence
3. **Atyantabhava** — absolute absence
4. **Anyonyabhava** — mutual absence/difference

### Timeline model

```text
Before production       Production          After destruction
      │                     │                       │
      ▼                     ▼                       ▼
Pragabhava              Bhava                Pradhvamsabhava
(prior absence)                              (posterior absence)
```

**Anyonyabhava** is conceptually different: it expresses mutual non-identity/difference between two things.

---

# 25. Why students confuse Padartha and Darshana

### Common error 1
“Padartha means only a substance.”

**Correction:** Dravya is one Padartha; Padartha is the broader category/framework.

### Common error 2
“All six Darshanas have exactly the same Padartha classification.”

**Correction:** Each system has its own conceptual vocabulary.

### Common error 3
“Nyaya and Vaisheshika are identical.”

**Correction:** They are closely related historical-philosophical traditions, but their emphases and category systems differ.

### Common error 4
“Sankhya's 25 Tattvas are the same as Vaisheshika's seven Padarthas.”

**Correction:** They are different classificatory frameworks.
