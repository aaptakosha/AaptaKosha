## 2. Meaning and etymological derivation of Guna

**Guna (गुण)** is derived from the Sanskrit verbal/root tradition associated with qualities, attributes and properties. In Padartha Vijnanam, Guna is a technical category rather than merely a moral virtue.

A concise teaching definition is:

> **Guna is that which resides in Dravya, is not itself an independent substratum of another Guna, and contributes to the recognition and action of the Dravya.**

In the Vaisheshika framework, Guna is a Padartha that **inheres in Dravya** and is distinguished from Karma because Guna does not itself produce conjunction/disjunction as an action category.

### Dravya–Guna–Karma relationship

**Dravya → Ashraya (substratum)**  
**Guna → Ashrita (quality residing in Dravya)**  
**Karma → action/movement associated with Dravya**

Conceptual diagram:

**Dravya (substratum)**  
↓ supports  
**Guna (quality)**  
↓ helps determine  
**Karma / effect / practical expression**

Example: a medicinal substance is Dravya; its Guru and Snigdha properties are Guna; its Dosha-modifying and therapeutic activity is discussed through Karma and the integrated pharmacodynamic framework.

---

## 3. Lakshana of Guna

The classical Vaisheshika definition emphasizes that Guna:

- resides in Dravya,
- does not possess another Guna or Karma as its own substratum in the same manner as Dravya,
- is not the cause of conjunction/disjunction as Karma is,
- is apprehended as a property of a substance.

Ayurvedic teaching uses Guna more functionally: the qualities of a substance help predict its effect on Dosha, Dhatu, Mala and bodily functions.

### Important distinction

**Guna is not synonymous with Karma.**

| Guna | Karma |
|---|---|
| Quality/property | Action/activity |
| Resides in Dravya | Action performed by Dravya |
| Example: Guru, Snigdha | Example: movement/action |
| Helps explain tendency and effect | Describes activity/process |

## 4. Classification and enumeration according to different Darshanas

### 4.1 Vaisheshika

The Vaisheshika system traditionally recognizes **24 Gunas**. These include:

1. Rupa
2. Rasa
3. Gandha
4. Sparsha
5. Shabda
6. Sankhya
7. Parimana
8. Prithaktva
9. Samyoga
10. Vibhaga
11. Paratva
12. Aparatva
13. Buddhi
14. Sukha
15. Dukha
16. Iccha
17. Dvesha
18. Prayatna
19. Gurutva
20. Dravatva
21. Sneha
22. Samskara
23. Dharma
24. Adharma

Different teaching texts may arrange or explain these in slightly different sequence; the student should preserve the classical enumeration.

### 4.2 Charaka/Ayurvedic classification

Ayurveda develops a clinically oriented Guna framework. For this NCISM chapter the important total is:

**5 Sartha/Indriyartha + 20 Gurvadi + 10 Paradi + 6 Adhyatma = 41 Gunas.**

This is a functional Ayurvedic enumeration and should not be confused with the 24-Guna enumeration of Vaisheshika.

### Comparison

| Framework | Important enumeration | Orientation |
|---|---:|---|
| Vaisheshika | 24 | Philosophical/categorical |
| Ayurvedic 41-Guna framework | 41 | Ayurvedic, therapeutic and experiential |
| Sartha/Indriyartha | 5 | Sensory objects |
| Gurvadi | 20 | Opposed qualitative pairs |
| Paradi | 10 | Relational/practical qualities |
| Adhyatma | 6 | Psychological/self-related qualities |

# 5. Sartha Guna / Indriyartha Guna

The five Sartha Gunas are the five sensory objects:

1. Shabda
2. Sparsha
3. Rupa
4. Rasa
5. Gandha

They are called **Indriyartha** because they are objects of the respective sensory faculties.

| Guna | Indriya | General object/example |
|---|---|---|
| Shabda | Shrotrendriya | Sound |
| Sparsha | Tvagindriya | Touch |
| Rupa | Chakshurindriya | Form/colour |
| Rasa | Rasanendriya | Taste |
| Gandha | Ghranendriya | Odour |

### 5.1 Shabda

Shabda is sound, apprehended through the auditory faculty.

Examples:
- speech,
- music,
- mantra,
- environmental sound.

**Application:** sound may influence attention, emotional state and sensory experience. Traditional applications such as Mantra Chikitsa and music-related therapeutic practices are discussed within their appropriate Ayurvedic context.

### 5.2 Sparsha

Sparsha is tactile quality apprehended through the skin.

Examples:
- warm/cold,
- smooth/rough,
- soft/hard,
- touch sensation.

**Application:** Abhyanga and touch-based procedures demonstrate the importance of tactile experience, while the specific therapeutic effect depends on the Dravya, procedure and context.

### 5.3 Rupa

Rupa is form/visible appearance, apprehended through vision.

Examples:
- colour,
- shape,
- visible form.

**Application:** colour and visual form are sensory information and can assist observation of a substance or clinical sign, but diagnostic interpretation requires the complete examination.

### 5.4 Rasa

Rasa is taste apprehended through the gustatory faculty.

The six Ayurvedic Rasas are:
- Madhura
- Amla
- Lavana
- Katu
- Tikta
- Kashaya

Rasa is not identical to the five Indriyartha categories as an enumeration; rather, it is one of the five sensory objects and is further classified into six tastes in Ayurveda.

### 5.5 Gandha

Gandha is odour apprehended through the olfactory faculty.

Examples:
- pleasant/unpleasant smell,
- characteristic odour of a Dravya.

Traditional applications may include fragrant substances and Gandha-based sensory interventions.

### Sartha Guna conceptual diagram

**Shabda → ear**  
**Sparsha → skin**  
**Rupa → eye**  
**Rasa → tongue**  
**Gandha → nose**

# 6. Gurvadi Guna — twenty qualities

The Gurvadi Gunas are ten pairs of mutually opposite qualities:

1. Guru — Laghu
2. Manda — Tikshna
3. Shita — Ushna
4. Snigdha — Ruksha
5. Shlakshna — Khara
6. Sandra — Drava
7. Mridu — Kathina
8. Sthira — Sara
9. Sukshma — Sthula
10. Vishada — Picchila

These pairs are among the most important clinical tools in Ayurvedic reasoning.

## 6.1 Guru — Laghu

**Guru:** heavy, comparatively difficult to digest or process.  
**Laghu:** light, comparatively easy to digest/process.

Examples:
- Guru tendency: heavy, dense, rich foods.
- Laghu tendency: light foods and substances.

Clinical reasoning:
- Guru qualities tend to increase heaviness and may aggravate Kapha when excessive.
- Laghu qualities generally support lightness and may be useful where heaviness is undesirable.

**Important:** “Guru” does not mean that a substance has greater mass in the physics sense in every clinical situation.

## 6.2 Manda — Tikshna

**Manda:** dull, slow, mild.  
**Tikshna:** sharp, penetrating, intense.

Clinical relevance:
- Manda qualities may be useful where excessive intensity is undesirable.
- Tikshna qualities may promote stronger penetration/action and may aggravate Pitta or irritative tendencies when excessive.

## 6.3 Shita — Ushna

**Shita:** cold/cooling.  
**Ushna:** hot/heating.

Clinical relevance:
- Ushna generally tends to reduce Kapha and Vata-related coldness but may increase Pitta when excessive.
- Shita may pacify heat/Pitta tendencies but can increase Kapha and Vata tendencies in susceptible contexts.

## 6.4 Snigdha — Ruksha

**Snigdha:** unctuous, oily, moist.  
**Ruksha:** dry, non-unctuous.

Clinical relevance:
- Snigdha tends to counter excessive dryness and may support Vata management.
- Ruksha tends to reduce excessive unctuousness but can aggravate Vata if excessive.

## 6.5 Shlakshna — Khara

**Shlakshna:** smooth.  
**Khara:** rough.

Examples:
- Shlakshna: smooth, non-abrasive texture.
- Khara: rough, abrasive tendency.

Clinical relevance includes texture, scraping-like effects and the condition of tissues or substances.

## 6.6 Sandra — Drava

**Sandra:** concentrated, compact, dense.  
**Drava:** liquid, flowing.

Clinical relevance:
- Sandra contributes to compactness.
- Drava contributes to liquidity and flow.

These qualities must be interpreted in relation to the complete Dravya and its other properties.

## 6.7 Mridu — Kathina

**Mridu:** soft, yielding.  
**Kathina:** hard, resistant.

Applications include tissue feel, food texture, procedural qualities and the nature of substances.

## 6.8 Sthira — Sara

**Sthira:** stable, steady, immobile.  
**Sara:** mobile, flowing, promoting movement.

Clinical reasoning:
- Sthira suggests stability.
- Sara suggests mobility or movement.

## 6.9 Sukshma — Sthula

**Sukshma:** subtle, minute, capable of penetrating fine channels.  
**Sthula:** gross, large, comparatively coarse.

Clinical relevance:
- Sukshma is important in traditional explanations of subtle penetration.
- Sthula is associated with grossness and bulk.

These are classical qualitative concepts, not direct microscopic size measurements.

## 6.10 Vishada — Picchila

**Vishada:** clear/non-slimy, non-sticky.  
**Picchila:** slimy, sticky, viscid.

Clinical relevance:
- Picchila is associated with stickiness and coating.
- Vishada is associated with clarity/non-stickiness.

# 8. Paradi Guna — ten qualities

The ten Paradi Gunas are:

1. Para
2. Apara
3. Yukti
4. Sankhya
5. Samyoga
6. Vibhaga
7. Prithaktva
8. Parimana
9. Samskara
10. Abhyasa

They are important because many are **relational, contextual or procedural** rather than simple sensory qualities.

## 8.1 Para

Para indicates superiority, priority or greater relevance in a given context.

Example:
- One factor may be more important than another for a specific therapeutic decision.

## 8.2 Apara

Apara indicates inferiority, posterior status or comparatively lesser relevance.

Para and Apara are understood relationally; the same factor may not be “para” in every context.

## 8.3 Yukti

Yukti is rational combination/planning based on multiple factors.

Clinical example:
- Selection of a treatment by considering Dosha, Bala, Kala, Desha, Agni, age and disease stage together.

**Yukti = integration of multiple relevant factors for a practical decision.**

## 8.4 Sankhya

Sankhya means number/count.

Examples:
- three Doshas,
- seven Dhatus,
- six Rasas,
- five Mahabhutas.

Enumeration is not merely memorization; it structures Ayurvedic reasoning.

## 8.5 Samyoga

Samyoga means conjunction or combination.

Examples:
- combination of ingredients in a formulation,
- association of two entities.

## 8.6 Vibhaga

Vibhaga means separation/disjunction.

Examples:
- separation of constituents,
- removal of an association.

## 8.7 Prithaktva

Prithaktva means distinctness/separateness.

It allows different entities to be recognized as separate rather than identical.

## 8.8 Parimana

Parimana means measurement/dimension/size.

Clinical examples:
- appropriate dose,
- quantity of food,
- dimensions relevant to a procedure.

## 8.9 Samskara

Samskara refers to processing/transformation that produces a change in the condition or properties relevant to use.

Examples in Ayurvedic pharmacy and food processing:
- heating,
- roasting,
- grinding,
- washing,
- soaking,
- repeated processing where prescribed.

The exact effect depends on the substance and process.

## 8.10 Abhyasa

Abhyasa means repetition or habitual practice.

Examples:
- regular exercise,
- repeated dietary habit,
- repeated therapeutic practice.

Abhyasa may establish a stable tendency or adaptation through repetition.

# 9. Adhyatma Guna — six qualities

The six Adhyatma Gunas are:

1. Iccha — desire
2. Dvesha — aversion
3. Sukha — pleasure
4. Dukha — pain/suffering
5. Prayatna — effort
6. Buddhi — cognition/intellect

These are discussed as qualities associated with the self/psychological domain in the relevant philosophical framework.

| Adhyatma Guna | Meaning | Simple example |
|---|---|---|
| Iccha | Desire | Desire for food |
| Dvesha | Aversion | Avoidance of an unpleasant stimulus |
| Sukha | Pleasure | Sense of well-being |
| Dukha | Suffering/pain | Distress or unpleasant experience |
| Prayatna | Effort | Voluntary attempt to act |
| Buddhi | Cognition/intellect | Judgement/knowledge |

### Important distinction

Adhyatma Gunas are not equivalent to modern psychiatric diagnostic categories. They are technical philosophical concepts.

# 11. Gunapradhanyata — importance of predominant Guna

**Gunapradhanyata** means recognizing which Guna is predominant and therefore most influential in a particular Dravya or situation.

A substance does not usually act through one isolated property. It has a **Guna profile**.

Conceptual model:

**Dravya → multiple Gunas → relative predominance → Dosha interaction → effect**

### Example

Suppose a substance has:
- Guru,
- Snigdha,
- Ushna,
- Sara qualities.

The clinical effect cannot be predicted from Guru alone. The complete profile and the patient's condition must be considered.

### Why predominance matters

The predominant Guna may:
- determine the direction of an effect,
- modify Dosha,
- influence Agni,
- influence tissue state,
- alter the suitability of a substance for a particular patient.

---

# 12. Guna and Dosha

A classical therapeutic principle is that similar qualities tend to increase a Dosha and opposing qualities tend to reduce it.

### General teaching table

| Dosha | Important qualitative tendencies | Opposing therapeutic direction |
|---|---|---|
| Vata | Ruksha, Laghu, Shita, Sukshma, Sara | Snigdha, Guru, Ushna, Sthira |
| Pitta | Ushna, Tikshna, Drava, Sara, Sukshma | Shita, Manda, etc. according to context |
| Kapha | Guru, Snigdha, Shita, Manda, Sthira, Picchila | Laghu, Ruksha, Ushna, Tikshna, Sara |

This is a **reasoning framework**, not a substitute for the complete Ayurvedic assessment.

# 13. Guna in Ahara

Guna helps explain why different foods produce different effects.

### Example framework

**Ahara Dravya → Rasa + Guna + Virya + Vipaka + Prabhava + Matra + Kala + individual factors → effect**

Examples:
- A heavy, unctuous food may increase heaviness and Kapha tendency in excess.
- A dry, light food may be unsuitable in an individual with marked Vata aggravation if taken excessively.
- A hot, sharp substance may be unsuitable in a person with strong Pitta aggravation.

Therefore, **Guna-based diet advice is individualized**.

---

# 14. Guna in Aushadha

Medicinal substances are selected using their integrated pharmacological qualities.

A useful Ayurvedic reasoning sequence is:

**Dravya → Rasa → Guna → Virya → Vipaka → Prabhava → Dosha/Karma → therapeutic decision**

Guna is therefore one component of a broader assessment.

### Why the same substance can be appropriate in one person and inappropriate in another

Consider:
- Prakriti,
- Vikriti,
- Agni,
- Bala,
- age,
- season,
- place,
- dose,
- route,
- disease stage,
- concomitant substances.

---

# 15. Guna in Dosha, Dhatu and Mala

Guna is not limited to medicinal substances.

### Dosha

Guna helps describe the functional nature of Vata, Pitta and Kapha.

### Dhatu

The qualities of tissues and their state can be discussed through qualitative assessment.

### Mala

The nature and movement of waste products may also be interpreted qualitatively.

### Clinical principle

**Observation → identification of dominant qualities → assessment of imbalance → selection of opposing/appropriate qualities.**

---

# 16. Guna and Kala

Seasonal changes alter environmental qualities and therefore influence the body.

Conceptual diagram:

**Ritu/season → environmental Guna → Agni/Dosha response → dietary and lifestyle adaptation**

Examples:
- cold seasons may emphasize Shita qualities,
- hot seasons may emphasize Ushna qualities,
- dry environmental conditions may emphasize Ruksha tendencies.

The exact Ayurvedic seasonal interpretation should follow the relevant Ritu and Dosha framework rather than a simplistic one-to-one rule.

---

# 17. Guna and Desha

Desha means place/region and can influence the predominance of environmental qualities.

Examples:
- dry/arid environment may emphasize Ruksha qualities,
- humid/marshy environments may show different qualitative tendencies.

Clinical reasoning:

**Desha → environmental qualities → patient adaptation → therapeutic selection**

---

# 18. Guna and Agni

Guna is important in understanding digestion and metabolism.

Examples:
- excessive Guru and Snigdha intake may challenge digestion in susceptible individuals,
- appropriately selected Laghu substances may be useful when lightness is desired,
- Tikshna/Ushna qualities may have strong digestive implications but may aggravate Pitta in excess.

Guna must therefore be integrated with Agni, not used as a stand-alone rule.

---

# 19. Guna and procedures

Many Ayurvedic procedures can be explained through their intended qualitative direction.

| Procedure/approach | Qualitative reasoning |
|---|---|
| Abhyanga | Snigdha/unctuous direction |
| Swedana | Ushna/warming direction |
| Rukshana | Ruksha/drying direction |
| Brimhana | Nourishing/building direction |
| Langhana | Lightening/reducing direction |

The procedure, material, dose and indication determine the actual therapeutic effect.

# 21. Contemporary science: how to use it correctly

NCISM specifically asks students to discuss principles and examples from contemporary sciences that enhance understanding of Guna.

These comparisons should be used as **learning analogies**, not as claims that modern science has experimentally proven the classical Guna theory.

### Examples

| Classical concept | Helpful contemporary analogy | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Guru/Laghu | density, composition, digestive load | not identical to mass/density |
| Ushna/Shita | thermal properties | not identical to temperature alone |
| Drava/Sandra | fluidity/viscosity | classical meaning is broader |
| Sukshma/Sthula | fine/coarse scale | not simply microscopic size |
| Samskara | processing-induced change | classical processing has specific context |
| Parimana | measurement/dose | classical context includes therapeutic appropriateness |
| Samyoga | combination | philosophical conjunction is broader than chemical bonding |

### Critical exam point

**Do not write “Gurvadi Guna are physical properties measured by modern physics.”**

Instead write:

> Contemporary scientific concepts may provide analogies that help students visualize certain classical qualities, but the Ayurvedic Guna framework remains a distinct conceptual system.

# 22. Guna and Rasa — do not confuse them

Rasa has two important uses in Ayurveda:

1. **Rasa as one of the five Sartha/Indriyartha Gunas** — taste.
2. **Rasa as the sixfold Ayurvedic taste classification** — Madhura, Amla, Lavana, Katu, Tikta, Kashaya.

Therefore:

**Sartha Guna includes Rasa.**

But:

**Six Rasas are a separate Ayurvedic classification of taste.**

---

# 23. Guna and Virya

Guna and Virya are related but not identical.

- **Guna:** quality/property.
- **Virya:** potency/action-producing capacity discussed in Ayurvedic pharmacology.

A substance may have a particular Guna profile and also be classified according to Virya.

Do not replace one category with another.

---

# 24. Guna and Karma

Guna helps explain **what a substance is like**; Karma explains **what it does**.

Example:

**Snigdha Guna → tendency to counter dryness**

The actual therapeutic **Karma** depends on the whole substance and clinical context.

This distinction is essential for examinations.

# 26. Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Correct understanding |
|---|---|
| Guna means only “good quality” | Guna is a technical category of property |
| Guna and Karma are identical | Guna is quality; Karma is action |
| There are only 20 Gunas in Ayurveda | NCISM teaches the 41-Guna framework |
| The 41 Gunas are the same as Vaisheshika's 24 | They are different enumerative frameworks |
| Guru means only high physical weight | Guru is a broader qualitative concept |
| Ushna means only measured temperature | It is a classical qualitative category |
| Sartha Guna means six Rasas | Sartha Guna are the five sensory objects |
| Rasa in Sartha Guna and six Rasas are unrelated | Six Rasas further classify the taste category |
| One Guna alone determines treatment | Clinical effect depends on the integrated profile and context |
| Modern science proves Gurvadi Guna | Modern concepts can provide analogies, not proof of identity |

# 27. Examination-ready definitions

### Define Guna

Guna is a Padartha that resides in Dravya and functions as its quality/property; in Vaisheshika it is distinct from Dravya and Karma and does not independently serve as the substratum of another Guna or Karma.

### Define Gurvadi Guna

Gurvadi Gunas are twenty qualities arranged into ten mutually opposing pairs and are important for understanding the qualitative and therapeutic effects of substances.

### Define Gunapradhanyata

Gunapradhanyata is the recognition of the predominance or greater functional importance of a particular Guna within the qualitative profile of a Dravya or clinical situation.

### Define Yukti

Yukti is rational planning or integration of multiple factors to arrive at an appropriate practical conclusion.

# 31. Case-based application

### Case 1 — excessive heaviness

A person regularly consumes very heavy, unctuous food and develops a tendency toward heaviness and sluggish digestion.

**Reasoning:** Guru and Snigdha qualities are prominent. Management should consider the complete clinical picture and appropriate opposing qualities.

### Case 2 — dryness

A person has marked dryness and irregularity with Vata aggravation.

**Reasoning:** Ruksha and related Vata-promoting qualities may be prominent. Snigdha and other appropriate opposing qualities may be considered according to the complete assessment.

### Case 3 — heat and irritability

A person has marked heat and Pitta aggravation.

**Reasoning:** Ushna and Tikshna tendencies may be relevant. Shita and Manda directions may be considered where clinically appropriate.

### Case 4 — selection of a formulation

Two formulations appear useful. One is more appropriate because the patient's age, Agni, Dosha, season and disease stage differ from another patient.

**Reasoning:** This demonstrates Gunapradhanyata and Yukti rather than mechanical application of one Guna.

# 37. Summary

Guna is a fundamental Padartha that explains the qualities of Dravya. Vaisheshika presents a philosophical enumeration of 24 Gunas, while Ayurveda develops a clinically useful 41-Guna framework for first-professional study: five Sartha Gunas, twenty Gurvadi Gunas, ten Paradi Gunas and six Adhyatma Gunas.

The Gurvadi pairs are especially important because their opposing nature provides a practical basis for qualitative reasoning. Paradi Gunas add relational and procedural dimensions, while Adhyatma Gunas address desire, aversion, pleasure, suffering, effort and cognition.

Gunapradhanyata prevents simplistic reasoning: treatment should not be selected from one isolated quality. Guna must be interpreted with the complete Dravya profile, Rasa, Virya, Vipaka, Karma, Dosha, Agni, Prakriti, Vikriti, Kala, Desha, Matra and patient factors.

Contemporary science can provide useful analogies for selected concepts, but classical Guna and modern scientific properties should not be declared identical without evidence.
