# Chapter 12 — Pratyaksha Pariksha / Pramana

## NCISM First Professional BAMS — Padartha Vijnanam

### Chapter scope

**Pratyaksha** is direct perception and is one of the principal means of valid knowledge. In Ayurveda and Darshana, the study of Pratyaksha includes not only the ordinary act of seeing or sensing, but also the nature of perception, its classifications, the relationship between Indriya and their objects, Sannikarsha, the role of Manas and other Karana, obstacles to perception, and methods of improving accurate observation.

The official NCISM AyUG-PV curriculum requires study of the **Lakshana and types of Pratyaksha**, including **Nirvikalpaka and Savikalpaka**, **Laukika and Alaukika** perception and their further classifications; **Indriya-prapyakaritva**; the six types of **Sannikarsha**; characteristics, classification and enumeration of Indriya; **Panchapanchaka**, Panchabhautikatva and Tulyayonitva of Indriya; **Trayodasha Karana** and dominance of Antahkarana; and hindrances and enhancement of direct perception. citeturn0search0turn0search1

# 2. Meaning of Pratyaksha

**Pratyaksha (प्रत्यक्ष)** literally conveys the idea of being directly before or present to perception.

In epistemology, Pratyaksha is **direct perceptual cognition** obtained through appropriate interaction between the knower, sensory apparatus, object and necessary internal conditions.

### Simple formula

**Object + Indriya + appropriate Sannikarsha + Manas/Antahkarana + proper conditions → perceptual cognition**

Pratyaksha is therefore more than simply “seeing.”

---

# 3. Lakshana of Pratyaksha

A student-level definition is:

**Pratyaksha is valid cognition arising directly through appropriate perceptual contact/conditions, without dependence on an inferential step as the immediate means of cognition.**

Different Darshanas formulate the exact definition differently.

### Important point

Perception can be affected by:

- defective sense organs;
- inadequate light;
- distance;
- obstruction;
- distraction;
- unsuitable attention;
- ambiguity;
- abnormal sensory conditions.

Therefore, not every sensory cognition is automatically **Prama**.

---

# 4. Pratyaksha as Pramana

Pratyaksha is accepted as a Pramana in major Indian epistemological traditions, although the detailed definition and classification differ.

Its practical importance is obvious:

A physician must directly observe the patient before forming a clinical conclusion.

Examples:

- complexion;
- posture;
- gait;
- speech;
- visible lesions;
- swelling;
- movement;
- touch-related findings;
- other directly examinable signs.

---

# 5. Types of Pratyaksha — broad map

The NCISM syllabus requires:

### A. Nirvikalpaka and Savikalpaka

- **Nirvikalpaka** — indeterminate/non-conceptual stage of perception.
- **Savikalpaka** — determinate/conceptually qualified perception.

### B. Laukika and Alaukika

- **Laukika Pratyaksha** — ordinary perception.
- **Alaukika Pratyaksha** — extraordinary perception.

Alaukika perception is further discussed through:

1. Samanyalakshana Pratyaksha
2. Jnanalakshana Pratyaksha
3. Yogaja Pratyaksha

---

# 6. Nirvikalpaka Pratyaksha

**Nirvikalpaka** is the indeterminate stage of perceptual cognition.

The object is apprehended without the full conceptual determination that characterizes a later judgment.

### Example

A person sees an unfamiliar object before identifying:

- what it is;
- its name;
- its specific characteristics.

The initial perceptual awareness is explained as Nirvikalpaka.

### Key idea

**Perception before full conceptual determination.**

---

# 7. Savikalpaka Pratyaksha

**Savikalpaka** is determinate perception in which the object is cognized with relevant attributes or conceptual qualification.

### Example

Instead of merely seeing an object, the observer recognizes:

> “This is a blue pot.”

The cognition includes:

- object;
- identity;
- attribute.

### Key idea

**Perception with determination/qualification.**

---

# 8. Nirvikalpaka versus Savikalpaka

| Feature | Nirvikalpaka | Savikalpaka |
|---|---|---|
| Cognition | Indeterminate | Determinate |
| Conceptual qualification | Not fully developed | Present |
| Example | Initial awareness of object | “This is a blue pot” |
| Clinical analogy | Raw observation | Identified finding |

### Clinical importance

A clinician may first observe a finding and then classify it.

This illustrates the practical movement from:

**observation → determination.**

---

# 9. Laukika Pratyaksha

**Laukika** means ordinary perception.

It occurs through ordinary relationships between:

- Indriya;
- object;
- appropriate perceptual conditions.

### Examples

- seeing a visible object;
- hearing a sound;
- smelling an odour;
- tasting a substance;
- touching an object.

This is the most familiar form of perception.

---

# 10. Alaukika Pratyaksha

**Alaukika** means extraordinary/non-ordinary perception.

Classically, it is described through special forms of perceptual cognition that do not operate in the ordinary sensory manner.

The three commonly discussed types are:

1. Samanyalakshana
2. Jnanalakshana
3. Yogaja

These belong to the philosophical analysis of perception and should not be casually equated with modern paranormal claims.

---

# 11. Samanyalakshana Pratyaksha

This is extraordinary perception involving cognition of a universal/general feature through perception of an individual.

### Concept

The observer perceives an individual and cognizes the relevant generality associated with it.

### Simple example

From perception of an individual cow, the universal **cowness** is apprehended in the philosophical analysis.

### Key idea

**Individual → cognition of universal/general feature.**

---

# 12. Jnanalakshana Pratyaksha

In **Jnanalakshana**, a previous cognition/knowledge contributes to a special perceptual cognition of an associated quality or object.

### Conceptual example

The cognition of an object can be associated with knowledge of a property that is not being perceived through the ordinary independent sensory route at that moment.

The precise examples vary across philosophical texts.

### Important caution

This is a technical philosophical classification and should not be interpreted as an assertion that memory literally creates a new physical sensory signal.

---

# 13. Yogaja Pratyaksha

**Yogaja Pratyaksha** is extraordinary perception associated with advanced yogic cognition in classical philosophical discussions.

It is described as arising through extraordinary yogic capacity.

### Examination point

Yogaja Pratyaksha is a classical epistemological concept.

It should not be presented as experimentally established modern sensory physiology.

---

# 14. Laukika and Alaukika — comparison

| Feature | Laukika | Alaukika |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Ordinary | Extraordinary |
| Typical mechanism | Ordinary sensory relation | Special philosophical mechanism |
| Types | Ordinary sensory perception | Samanyalakshana, Jnanalakshana, Yogaja |
| Clinical use | Directly central | Primarily philosophical/epistemological |

---

# 15. Indriya-prapyakaritva

**Indriya-prapyakaritva** concerns the question of whether the sensory organ reaches/comes into appropriate relation with its object in perception.

The concept is important because perception requires an appropriate relationship between:

**Indriya ↔ Artha**

Different Darshanas analyse this relationship differently.

### Practical principle

Accurate perception requires an appropriate interaction between the sensory apparatus and the object under suitable conditions.

---

# 16. Indriya and Artha

The five Jnanendriya and their objects are:

| Indriya | Artha |
|---|---|
| Chakshu | Rupa |
| Shrotra | Shabda |
| Ghrana | Gandha |
| Rasana | Rasa |
| Tvak | Sparsha |

### Memory

**Shabda – Shrotra**

**Sparsha – Tvak**

**Rupa – Chakshu**

**Rasa – Rasana**

**Gandha – Ghrana**

---

# 17. Sannikarsha

**Sannikarsha** means the relation/contact between the sense organ and its object that enables perceptual cognition in the classical Nyaya analysis.

Six types are traditionally described.

---

# 18. Six types of Sannikarsha

1. **Samyoga**
2. **Samyukta-Samavaya**
3. **Samyukta-Samaveta-Samavaya**
4. **Samavaya**
5. **Samaveta-Samavaya**
6. **Visheshaṇa-Visheshya-Bhava**

These relationships provide a detailed philosophical account of how different kinds of objects and properties become objects of perception.

---

# 19. Samyoga

**Samyoga** means conjunction/contact.

### Example

The eye is in relation with an external visible object.

This is the basic relation through which the external object becomes available for perception.

### Memory

**Samyoga = conjunction.**

---

# 20. Samyukta-Samavaya

This refers to inherence of a property in an object that is itself in conjunction with the sense organ.

### Example

A visible colour inheres in an object, and that object is in conjunction with the eye.

Thus:

**Eye → object (Samyoga)**

**Colour → object (Samavaya)**

This combined relation is **Samyukta-Samavaya**.

---

# 21. Samyukta-Samaveta-Samavaya

This is a further relational chain.

A property inheres in another property/entity that is itself related through the preceding relations.

A simplified conceptual chain is:

**Sense organ → object → property → universal/property of that property**

The classical analysis uses this structure to explain perception of increasingly remote relational objects.

### Examination point

Memorize the sequence and understand the relational chain rather than treating the term as an isolated word.

---

# 22. Samavaya

In this context, **Samavaya** refers to inherence.

A sense organ may be directly related to an entity through an inherence relation in the philosophical model.

### Important distinction

Samavaya is not the same as ordinary conjunction.

This connects directly with the previous chapter on Samavaya.

---

# 23. Samaveta-Samavaya

This is another higher-order inherence relation used in the classical analysis of perception.

The exact object of cognition is connected through:

**inherence → inherence**

The classification demonstrates the sophisticated relational analysis developed in Nyaya epistemology.

---

# 24. Visheshaṇa-Visheshya-Bhava

This is the qualifier-qualified relationship.

It is especially relevant to cognition of absence/negation.

### Example

Perceiving:

> “There is no pot on the floor.”

The floor is apprehended as the locus and the absence functions as the qualifier in the classical analysis.

### Important connection

This Sannikarsha links the study of **Pratyaksha** with the previous chapter on **Abhava**.

---

# 25. Six Sannikarsha — master table

| No. | Sannikarsha | Basic idea |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Samyoga | Conjunction |
| 2 | Samyukta-Samavaya | Inherence in the conjoined object |
| 3 | Samyukta-Samaveta-Samavaya | Further relational inherence |
| 4 | Samavaya | Inherence |
| 5 | Samaveta-Samavaya | Inherence in an inhering entity |
| 6 | Visheshaṇa-Visheshya-Bhava | Qualifier-qualified relation |

# 26. Indriya — meaning

**Indriya** are the faculties/instruments through which sensory cognition is obtained.

Ayurvedic and philosophical literature discusses Indriya in different contexts.

For Pratyaksha, the focus is primarily on the **Jnanendriya**.

---

# 27. Enumeration of Jnanendriya

Five Jnanendriya are:

1. Shrotra
2. Tvak
3. Chakshu
4. Rasana
5. Ghrana

They correspond to:

1. Shabda
2. Sparsha
3. Rupa
4. Rasa
5. Gandha

# 28. Jnanendriya and Karmendriya

The broader ten Indriya framework includes:

### Five Jnanendriya

- Shrotra
- Tvak
- Chakshu
- Rasana
- Ghrana

### Five Karmendriya

- Vak
- Pani
- Pada
- Payu
- Upastha

### Important

Jnanendriya are associated with cognition.

Karmendriya are associated with action.

---

# 29. Panchapanchaka

**Panchapanchaka** is a fivefold organization involving five sets of five.

A useful study framework is:

1. five Jnanendriya;
2. five sensory objects;
3. five Mahabhuta;
4. five corresponding sensory capacities/functional relationships;
5. the associated elemental or philosophical correspondences as taught in the prescribed text.

Because exact presentation varies among Ayurvedic texts and teaching traditions, students should reproduce the institution's prescribed table in examination answers.

### Core memory

**Five Indriya + five Artha + five Mahabhuta = the central Panchapanchaka framework.**

---

# 30. Panchabhautikatva of Indriya

Ayurveda describes the Indriya as having a **Panchabhautik** basis.

This means the sensory apparatus is understood in relation to all five Mahabhuta, although a particular Indriya has a predominance/association with a particular Mahabhuta.

### Classical predominance commonly taught

| Indriya | Predominant Mahabhuta association |
|---|---|
| Shrotra | Akasha |
| Tvak | Vayu |
| Chakshu | Tejas |
| Rasana | Jala |
| Ghrana | Prithvi |

### Important

“Predominant association” should not be confused with exclusive composition.

---

# 31. Tulyayonitva

**Tulyayonitva** refers to similarity in source/origin between an Indriya and its corresponding sensory object.

A commonly taught example is:

- Shrotra ↔ Shabda ↔ Akasha
- Tvak ↔ Sparsha ↔ Vayu
- Chakshu ↔ Rupa ↔ Tejas
- Rasana ↔ Rasa ↔ Jala
- Ghrana ↔ Gandha ↔ Prithvi

The idea is that similarity of elemental basis supports sensory cognition.

---

# 34. Antahkarana

**Antahkarana** means the internal instrument.

It is commonly discussed through:

- Manas;
- Buddhi;
- Ahamkara;
- and, in some philosophical formulations, Chitta.

Different Darshanas organize these internal functions differently.

For the NCISM-level study of Pratyaksha, the important principle is:

**Sensory information requires internal coordination before determinate cognition becomes possible.**

---

# 35. Dominance of Antahkarana

The external Indriya cannot produce meaningful cognition independently of internal coordination.

### Simplified process

**Indriya receives/relates to object**

↓

**Manas attends and coordinates**

↓

**Buddhi determines**

↓

**Ahamkara relates cognition to the experiencer**

↓

**Cognition becomes available for action/knowledge**

This is a conceptual teaching model, not a direct anatomical diagram.

---

# 36. Manas and perception

Manas is important because simultaneous perception through all senses does not occur indiscriminately in ordinary cognition.

Attention is selective.

This classical insight can be used pedagogically to understand:

- attention;
- distraction;
- selective perception;
- cognitive overload.

### Important

Modern neuroscience can provide analogies, but it should not be presented as proof of the classical metaphysical identity of Manas.

---

# 37. Causes of failure of Pratyaksha

The syllabus specifically requires study of **Pratyaksha-anupalabdhikaarana** — causes for failure/non-availability of direct perception. citeturn0search0

Direct perception may fail because of:

- excessive distance;
- excessive proximity;
- obstruction;
- inadequate illumination;
- very small or subtle object;
- excessive speed;
- defective sensory organ;
- absence of attention;
- mental distraction;
- similarity/confusion;
- non-existence of the object;
- temporary sensory limitation.

These factors demonstrate:

**Not perceived ≠ necessarily nonexistent.**

---

# 38. Distance

An object may be too far away to produce adequate sensory cognition.

### Example

A distant small object may not be visually identified.

This does not prove that it does not exist.

---

# 39. Excessive proximity

Very close positioning can also interfere with clear perception.

### Example

A visual target may become difficult to focus on when placed outside the effective range of the visual system.

Thus both distance and proximity can matter.

---

# 40. Obstruction

A barrier may prevent sensory access.

### Example

An object behind a wall is not visually perceived.

Its non-perception does not establish its non-existence.

---

# 41. Inadequate illumination

Poor light can prevent accurate visual perception.

### Clinical analogy

A skin lesion may be missed under poor lighting.

Improving illumination improves the reliability of observation.

---

# 42. Subtlety and smallness

Very small or subtle objects may escape ordinary perception.

Modern microscopy and imaging can reveal features that unaided senses cannot.

This illustrates:

**limits of unaided perception are not equivalent to absence of the object.**

---

# 43. Excessive speed

A rapidly moving object may not be clearly perceived.

This is especially relevant to:

- movement;
- transient clinical signs;
- rapidly changing physiological states.

Repeated or instrument-assisted observation can improve detection.

---

# 44. Defective Indriya

Sensory impairment may reduce the accuracy of perception.

Examples:

- reduced vision;
- hearing impairment;
- altered taste;
- impaired smell;
- reduced tactile sensation.

The observer's sensory condition must therefore be considered.

---

# 45. Lack of attention

An object may be present but not consciously registered because attention is directed elsewhere.

### Clinical example

A clinician may overlook a subtle sign while focusing on another prominent complaint.

This is a major reason for systematic examination.

---

# 46. Improving Pratyaksha

Direct perception can be enhanced by improving the conditions of observation.

### Methods

1. Adequate illumination.
2. Appropriate distance.
3. Correct positioning.
4. Repeated observation.
5. Appropriate sensory examination.
6. Attention and concentration.
7. Proper examination technique.
8. Appropriate instruments.
9. Cross-checking with another observer where appropriate.
10. Correlation with history and other Pramanas.

---

# 47. Pratyaksha in clinical examination

Direct observation is central to:

- general examination;
- inspection;
- palpation;
- auscultation;
- assessment of gait;
- speech observation;
- visible lesions;
- external signs;
- sensory findings.

The exact examination method depends on the clinical question.

---

# 48. Pratyaksha in Nidana

A diagnostic examination can begin with direct findings.

### Example

A patient presents with a skin lesion.

**Pratyaksha:**

- colour;
- location;
- size;
- shape;
- surface;
- distribution;
- discharge;
- tenderness on appropriate examination.

Then:

**Anumana** interprets the significance.

**Yukti** integrates the complete pattern.

Thus Pratyaksha is essential but not sufficient for every diagnosis.

---

# 49. Pratyaksha in Chikitsa

Treatment response may also be directly observed.

Examples:

- reduction of swelling;
- change in colour;
- improvement in mobility;
- change in bowel pattern;
- change in appetite;
- wound appearance.

Repeated examination allows monitoring of change.

---

# 50. Pratyaksha and instruments

Contemporary clinical practice extends direct observation through instruments.

Examples include:

- thermometer;
- stethoscope;
- blood-pressure apparatus;
- microscope;
- imaging devices;
- laboratory measurement instruments.

These improve observation by extending or quantifying sensory access.

### Important philosophical caution

Instrument-mediated data should be interpreted through the appropriate epistemological framework and should not be simplistically equated with unaided classical Pratyaksha.

---

# 51. Pratyaksha versus Anumana

| Pratyaksha | Anumana |
|---|---|
| Direct perceptual cognition | Inferential cognition |
| Depends on perceptual conditions | Depends on relation/reasoning |
| Example: observing pallor | Inferring a possible underlying condition |
| Immediate perceptual basis | Conclusion beyond the directly perceived sign |

### Clinical sequence

**Observe → infer → integrate**

---

# 52. Pratyaksha versus Aptopadesha

| Pratyaksha | Aptopadesha |
|---|---|
| Direct observation | Reliable testimony |
| Current patient/object | Previously established instruction |
| Sensory examination | Verbal knowledge |
| “I observe this sign” | “The authoritative text describes this pattern” |

A competent clinician uses both appropriately.

---

# 54. Practical exercise — observation versus inference

### Finding

Patient has visible pallor.

**Pratyaksha:** Pallor is observed.

### Interpretation

The physician considers possible causes.

**Anumana:** An underlying condition may be inferred from the full evidence.

### Final decision

**Yukti:** Multiple factors are integrated.

This distinction prevents students from confusing an observation with a diagnosis.

# 57. Common misconceptions

### Misconception 1
**Pratyaksha means only seeing.**

**Correction:** It encompasses direct perceptual cognition through appropriate sensory processes.

### Misconception 2
**Everything sensed is automatically Prama.**

**Correction:** Perceptual errors and defective conditions can produce invalid cognition.

### Misconception 3
**If something is not perceived, it does not exist.**

**Correction:** Distance, obstruction, subtlety, sensory defects and other factors can prevent perception.

### Misconception 4
**Nirvikalpaka and Savikalpaka are two unrelated senses.**

**Correction:** They describe stages/forms of perceptual cognition in the philosophical framework.

### Misconception 5
**Yogaja Pratyaksha is modern scientific paranormal perception.**

**Correction:** It is a classical philosophical concept.

### Misconception 6
**Sannikarsha means only physical touching.**

**Correction:** It denotes specific philosophical relations involved in perception.

### Misconception 7
**Clinical diagnosis is always Pratyaksha.**

**Correction:** Diagnosis commonly requires Pratyaksha plus Anumana and Yukti.

# 62. Viva questions

**Q1. What is Pratyaksha?**  
Direct perceptual cognition.

**Q2. What is Nirvikalpaka?**

# 68. Summary

Pratyaksha is direct perceptual cognition and a major Pramana in Indian epistemology and Ayurveda. Its study extends beyond ordinary sensory observation to the philosophical analysis of how perception occurs.

The major classifications include **Nirvikalpaka and Savikalpaka**, and **Laukika and Alaukika** Pratyaksha. Alaukika Pratyaksha is traditionally divided into **Samanyalakshana, Jnanalakshana and Yogaja**.

The classical Nyaya analysis of perception explains the relationship between Indriya and object through six forms of **Sannikarsha**. Ayurveda additionally emphasizes the Indriya, their Panchabhautik basis, Tulyayonitva and the role of internal instruments within the broader Trayodasha Karana framework.

Direct perception can fail because of distance, obstruction, inadequate conditions, subtlety, rapid movement, sensory defects, lack of attention and other factors. Therefore, **non-perception does not automatically establish non-existence**.

In clinical practice, Pratyaksha supplies direct findings. These findings are then interpreted through **Anumana and Yukti**, while appropriate authoritative knowledge provides the conceptual framework.

The central practical lesson is:

**Good perception requires an appropriate object, functioning sensory apparatus, suitable conditions, attention and disciplined observation.**
