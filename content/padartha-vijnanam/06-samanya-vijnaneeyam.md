# Chapter 6 — Samanya Vijnaneeyam

## NCISM First Professional BAMS — Padartha Vijnanam

### Chapter scope

This chapter covers **Samanya (generality/commonness)** as a Padartha and as an important principle of Ayurvedic reasoning. The NCISM syllabus places the topic under Padartha Vijnanam and expects four broad areas:

1. Introduction of the concept of Samanya according to Darshana and Ayurveda.
2. Classification of Samanya.
3. Practical application of Samanya.
4. Principles and examples from contemporary sciences that enhance understanding of Samanya.

---

## 1. Learning objectives

After completing this chapter, the learner should be able to:

- define Samanya and explain its derivation;
- describe the concept of Samanya in Darshana, especially Vaisheshika;
- explain the Ayurvedic understanding of Samanya;
- distinguish Samanya from Vishesha;
- explain why Samanya is associated with **Vriddhi** (increase);
- identify relevant forms of Samanya used in Ayurvedic clinical reasoning;
- apply Samanya to Dosha, Dhatu, Mala, Agni, Ahara and Aushadha;
- explain the role of Samanya in Brimhana and other therapeutic decisions;
- recognize the difference between philosophical Samanya and practical Ayurvedic Samanya;
- use contemporary scientific analogies without treating them as literal equivalence;
- answer SAQs, LAQs, viva questions and case-based questions on Samanya.

---

# 2. Meaning and etymology of Samanya

The Sanskrit term **Samanya (सामान्य)** is derived from the idea of being common, shared, similar or belonging together.

In philosophical discussion, Samanya expresses **commonness or generality** by which multiple individual entities are understood as sharing a common character.

In Ayurvedic reasoning, the concept becomes highly practical: things possessing similarity with a body component tend to contribute to its increase when the relevant conditions permit.

### Core idea

> **Samanya is the principle of commonness or similarity that explains a tendency toward increase.**

The word should not be reduced to the ordinary-language meaning of “general.” In Padartha Vijnanam it has a technical philosophical and epistemological significance.

---

# 3. Samanya as a Padartha

Different Darshanas use the concept of Samanya in different ways.

In the Vaisheshika system, Samanya is one of the categories used to account for common characteristics among particulars. It is associated with the recognition of a common nature across multiple individual entities.

A classic example is the commonness of **“cowness”** among individual cows. Individual cows are different particulars, yet they are recognized under a common class-character.

Thus:

**Individual objects → shared common character → recognition of a class/general**

This philosophical treatment provides the foundation for understanding why several entities can be grouped under a common feature.

---

# 4. Samanya in Darshana

## 4.1 Vaisheshika perspective

Vaisheshika accepts Samanya as a distinct category (Padartha). It is understood as a common property or universal that resides in multiple individual substances and permits recognition of commonality.

Important ideas include:

- Samanya is related to **anugata-pratyaya**, the cognition of a common feature running through multiple individuals.
- It is opposed conceptually to **Vishesha**, which accounts for particularity.
- Higher and lower forms of commonness can be discussed according to their range of application.
- A commonness may be shared by many entities, while a more specific commonness may apply to a narrower class.

### Example

Consider:

**Cow A, Cow B, Cow C**

They are different individuals. The common character by which they are recognized as belonging to the class “cow” illustrates Samanya.

---

## 4.2 Generality and particularity

A useful philosophical pair is:

**Samanya = commonness / generality**

**Vishesha = particularity / differentiation**

The two principles help us understand both:

- what entities have in common; and
- what makes one entity distinct from another.

This pair becomes especially important in Ayurveda because therapeutic reasoning requires both recognition of similarity and recognition of difference.

---

# 5. Ayurvedic concept of Samanya

Ayurveda gives Samanya a distinctly functional and therapeutic importance.

The central Ayurvedic proposition is expressed through the classical principle:

**“सर्वदा सर्वभावानां सामान्यं वृद्धिकारणम्”**

The teaching is that Samanya is a cause of increase of the corresponding entity, when the necessary conditions are present.

This should be understood functionally rather than mechanically.

### Practical meaning

If two factors share relevant qualities, administration or exposure to the appropriate factor can support increase of the corresponding body component.

Examples:

- nourishing food can support nourishment;
- appropriate use of similar qualities can support increase of deficient tissues;
- heavy and unctuous nutritional measures may support conditions requiring Brimhana;
- excessive exposure to similar Dosha-provoking qualities may increase that Dosha.

Therefore Samanya is a foundation for **therapeutic selection**.

---

# 6. Samanya and Vriddhi

The most important Ayurvedic relationship is:

**Samanya → Vriddhi**

Here, Vriddhi means increase or augmentation.

However, Samanya should not be interpreted as saying that every superficially similar thing always produces unlimited increase.

The actual outcome depends on:

- quantity;
- quality;
- strength of the individual;
- Agni;
- Kala;
- Desha;
- age;
- disease state;
- route and method of administration;
- dose and duration;
- interactions with other factors.

Thus the principle is a **causal therapeutic rule**, not a simplistic one-variable formula.

---

# 7. Types and classifications of Samanya

The exact terminology and classification can vary according to the Darshana and the context in which the concept is discussed. For BAMS study, it is useful to organize Samanya in two complementary ways.

## 7.1 Philosophical classification

Samanya may be understood according to the range of commonness:

- **Higher/general Samanya** — common to a broader group.
- **Lower/specific Samanya** — common to a narrower group.

For example, a very broad commonness can include a large class of entities, while a narrower class-character applies only to a more restricted group.

The important examination point is that philosophical classification is concerned with **generality and commonness**, whereas Ayurvedic therapeutic classification emphasizes **functional similarity and increase**.

## 7.2 Ayurvedic functional classification

In Ayurveda, Samanya may be applied according to the level at which similarity is observed:

### Dravya Samanya

Similarity at the level of substance.

Example: a nutrient substance that resembles or directly supplies the material relevant to a tissue.

### Guna Samanya

Similarity at the level of qualities.

Example: repeated intake of Guru and Snigdha substances may support an increase in qualities associated with heaviness and unctuousness.

### Karma Samanya

Similarity at the level of action or functional effect.

Example: repeated performance or exposure to an action having a similar functional tendency can reinforce that tendency.

These categories are useful for clinical reasoning, but they should not be confused with a claim that every classical text uses an identical three-part formal taxonomy in every context.

---

# 8. Samanya and Vishesha

Samanya and Vishesha should be learned together.

| Principle | Core meaning | Main functional direction |
|---|---|---|
| Samanya | Similarity/commonness | Increase |
| Vishesha | Difference/opposition | Decrease |

The Ayurvedic therapeutic logic can therefore be summarized as:

**Similar factors → increase**

**Opposite/dissimilar factors → decrease**

This is the conceptual foundation of the **Samanya–Vishesha Siddhanta**.

### Important qualification

The word “opposite” does not mean that every unrelated factor is automatically Vishesha. Therapeutic Vishesha requires a meaningful differentiating or opposing relationship relevant to the condition being treated.

---

# 9. Samanya–Vishesha Siddhanta

The principle is central to Ayurvedic diagnosis and management.

When a Dosha, Dhatu, Mala, Agni-related property or other physiological factor is deficient, appropriate similarity can be used to promote it.

When a Dosha or pathological property is excessive, appropriate dissimilarity can be used to reduce it.

### Clinical reasoning sequence

**Assess state → identify deficient/excessive factor → identify relevant qualities → select Samanya or Vishesha → choose Dravya/Guna/Karma → determine dose and method → monitor response**

This prevents the concept from being used as a crude “like cures like” slogan.

---

# 10. Samanya and Dravya

Dravya is the substance through which qualities and actions are available for therapeutic use.

A Dravya may contribute to increase because of:

- its own material similarity;
- its Gunas;
- its Karma;
- its combination of these factors.

### Example

A nourishing food may be selected when tissue nourishment is required. Its therapeutic suitability is not judged only by its name; its Guru, Snigdha, Madhura or other relevant attributes, digestibility, dose and the patient's Agni must also be considered.

Therefore:

**Dravya is the carrier of therapeutic potential; Samanya helps explain why a selected Dravya is appropriate.**

---

# 11. Samanya and Guna

Guna is one of the most important practical levels of Samanya.

If the desired outcome requires increase of a quality, exposure to a suitable source possessing a corresponding quality may support that increase.

### Examples

| Desired direction | Relevant similar qualities | Possible Ayurvedic application |
|---|---|---|
| Nourishment / Brimhana | Guru, Snigdha, stable/nourishing attributes | Appropriate Brimhana measures |
| Dryness reduction | Snigdha | Unctuous diet or therapy when indicated |
| Excess heaviness reduction | Laghu | Light, appropriately selected measures |
| Excess cold reduction | Ushna | Warming measures when appropriate |

These are examples of reasoning, not universal prescriptions.

---

# 12. Samanya and Karma

Karma refers to action.

A similarity of action can also be clinically meaningful. For example, repeated activity with a particular physiological direction may reinforce that direction.

This is why Ayurvedic management considers not only what a patient consumes but also:

- physical activity;
- sleep and wake patterns;
- exercise;
- rest;
- therapeutic procedures;
- occupational activity;
- behavioral patterns.

A complete Samanya analysis therefore asks:

**What is similar in substance, quality or action?**

---

# 13. Samanya and Dosha

Doshas are strongly influenced by similar and dissimilar qualities.

## Vata

Vata is associated with qualities such as Ruksha, Laghu, Sheeta, Khara, Sukshma and Chala.

Excessive exposure to similar qualities may aggravate Vata.

Therefore, where clinically appropriate, opposite qualities are selected to reduce Vata aggravation.

## Pitta

Pitta has prominent Ushna, Tikshna, Drava, Sara and related qualities.

Similar factors may increase Pitta.

Appropriate cooling and Pitta-pacifying measures use relevant dissimilarity.

## Kapha

Kapha has prominent Guru, Snigdha, Sheeta, Manda, Sthira and related qualities.

Repeated exposure to similar factors may increase Kapha.

Langhana and appropriately selected lighter or warming measures may be used when indicated.

### Exam point

Samanya helps explain **why similar qualities can aggravate a Dosha**, while Vishesha helps explain **why opposite qualities can pacify it**.

---

# 14. Samanya and Dhatu

The principle is particularly important in tissue nourishment.

When a Dhatu is depleted, treatment is selected according to:

- the nature of the Dhatu;
- its required nutrients and qualities;
- Agni;
- the patient's strength;
- disease state;
- digestion and metabolism;
- appropriate route and dose.

This is the practical basis of **Brimhana** reasoning.

### Example

If a patient has a state requiring nourishment, simply increasing food quantity is not sufficient. The selected food must be:

- digestible;
- suitable for the patient's Agni;
- appropriate for the disease;
- appropriate for the patient's constitution and strength;
- administered in a suitable quantity.

Thus Samanya operates within a larger clinical decision system.

---

# 15. Samanya and Mala

Mala are also influenced by dietary, behavioral and therapeutic factors.

For example, inappropriate similarity in diet and lifestyle can contribute to excessive accumulation of certain waste-related processes, while appropriate differentiation can help restore balance.

The principle should therefore be applied to the **state of the individual**, not to an isolated textbook label.

---

# 16. Samanya and Agni

Agni is a major modifier of therapeutic outcome.

A substance that is theoretically nourishing may fail to produce the intended benefit if:

- Agni is weak;
- digestion is impaired;
- the dose is excessive;
- the timing is inappropriate;
- the substance is incompatible with the individual.

Therefore:

**Samanya explains the direction of the intended effect; Agni and other factors influence whether that effect is actually achieved.**

---

# 17. Samanya in Ahara

Ahara is one of the clearest practical fields for understanding Samanya.

### Example: nutritional increase

A person requiring nourishment may receive an appropriately selected nourishing diet.

The reasoning involves:

1. identify the nutritional deficit;
2. identify required qualities;
3. identify suitable foods;
4. assess Agni;
5. select quantity;
6. select timing;
7. observe response.

### Important caution

“More similar food = more health” is incorrect.

Excessive similarity can produce excessive accumulation or Dosha aggravation.

Therefore the Ayurvedic principle is:

**appropriate similarity according to need and context.**

---

# 18. Samanya in Aushadha

Aushadha selection involves multiple layers:

**Roga → Rogi → Dosha → Dushya → Agni → Bala → Kala → Desha → Dravya/Guna/Karma → dose and route**

Samanya contributes to this decision by identifying useful similarities.

### Example

If a particular pathological quality is excessive, selecting an intervention with the same quality would generally be inappropriate unless there is a specific reason.

Conversely, if a deficient property needs support, a suitably similar intervention may be useful.

Thus Samanya is a **reasoning principle**, not a standalone prescription algorithm.

---

# 19. Samanya in Brimhana and Langhana

## Brimhana

Brimhana aims toward nourishment and building.

Samanya supports the selection of nourishing substances and qualities where increase is required.

## Langhana

Langhana aims toward lightening/reducing where indicated.

Here the therapeutic reasoning often relies more strongly on Vishesha or dissimilarity.

### Simplified contrast

**Deficient / depleted → consider appropriate Samanya**

**Excessive / aggravated → consider appropriate Vishesha**

This is only a conceptual starting point; actual treatment requires complete clinical assessment.

---

# 20. Samanya and Dosha-specific examples

| Clinical direction | Samanya reasoning | Therapeutic direction |
|---|---|---|
| Vata increase | Similar Vata-promoting qualities | Avoid excessive similarity; use appropriate opposite measures |
| Pitta increase | Similar Pitta-promoting qualities | Use suitable Pitta-pacifying measures |
| Kapha increase | Similar Kapha-promoting qualities | Use suitable Langhana/lightening measures |
| Dhatu depletion | Similar nourishing factors | Brimhana when indicated |
| Excess heaviness | Similar Guru factors would increase it | Select appropriate Laghu/dissimilar measures |
| Excess dryness | Similar Ruksha exposure may increase it | Select appropriate Snigdha measures |

---

# 21. Samanya and Kala

The same intervention may not produce the same outcome in every season or time.

Kala modifies:

- Dosha tendency;
- Agni;
- strength;
- digestion;
- environmental exposure;
- therapeutic suitability.

Therefore Samanya must be interpreted with Kala.

### Example

A heavy, unctuous and cooling intervention may have different effects in different seasons and in different individuals.

Hence:

**Samanya + Kala + Desha + Rogi Bala + Agni = clinically meaningful reasoning**

---

# 22. Samanya and Desha

Desha modifies the expression of qualities.

A substance that is suitable in one geographic or environmental context may be unsuitable in another.

Clinical application therefore asks:

- What is the patient's habitat?
- What is the environmental exposure?
- What is the patient's accustomed diet?
- What qualities are already dominant?

This prevents mechanical application of a single rule.

---

# 23. Samanya and Prakriti

Prakriti represents an individual's relatively stable constitutional pattern.

Because Prakriti is associated with particular Dosha proportions and qualities, Samanya helps explain why the same diet or behavior may affect two individuals differently.

### Example

A Kapha-dominant individual may be more susceptible to excessive exposure to Guru-Snigdha factors than a person with a different constitutional tendency.

Therefore Samanya should be individualized.

---

# 24. Samanya and Ritu

Seasonal regimens also illustrate the principle.

Ayurveda adjusts Ahara and Vihara according to seasonal changes in qualities and Dosha.

The learner should ask:

**Which qualities are increasing naturally in this season?**

Then:

**Should treatment reinforce or oppose that tendency?**

This is Samanya–Vishesha reasoning in practice.

---

# 25. Practical method for identifying Samanya

When given a clinical situation, use the following five-step method:

### Step 1 — Identify the target

What needs to increase or decrease?

### Step 2 — Identify its important qualities

For example:

- Guru
- Laghu
- Snigdha
- Ruksha
- Ushna
- Sheeta
- Sthira
- Chala

### Step 3 — Identify the intervention's qualities

What qualities are present in the food, drug, behavior or therapy?

### Step 4 — Compare

- Similar → Samanya
- Dissimilar/opposing → Vishesha

### Step 5 — Check modifying factors

Assess:

- dose;
- Agni;
- Kala;
- Desha;
- Bala;
- Prakriti;
- age;
- disease;
- route;
- duration.

---

# 26. Practical exercise 1 — Food qualities

### Situation

A person with marked heaviness and sluggishness repeatedly consumes very heavy, oily and sedentary lifestyle factors.

### Analysis

1. Target problem: excessive heaviness and Kapha-related tendency.
2. Existing qualities: Guru, Snigdha, Sthira and reduced activity.
3. Similarity: continued exposure reinforces the same direction.
4. Samanya therefore helps explain aggravation.
5. Appropriate Vishesha-based measures may be considered clinically.

### Learning point

Samanya explains both therapeutic increase and pathological aggravation.

---

# 27. Practical exercise 2 — Tissue nourishment

### Situation

A patient has a genuine need for nourishment after appropriate clinical assessment.

### Analysis

1. Identify nutritional requirement.
2. Assess Agni.
3. Select digestible nourishing substances.
4. Choose appropriate Guru/Snigdha or other required qualities.
5. Adjust quantity and timing.
6. Monitor response.

### Learning point

Similarity is useful only when it is **appropriate to the patient's actual state**.

---

# 28. Practical exercise 3 — Activity

A person with prolonged sedentary behavior develops excessive heaviness.

Repeated inactivity represents a behavioral factor with a similar functional tendency.

Introducing suitable activity can provide a differentiating effect.

### Learning point

Samanya and Vishesha apply not only to medicines but also to **Karma and lifestyle**.

---

# 29. Samanya, Samavaya and other Padarthas

Do not confuse Samanya with Samavaya.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Samanya | Generality/commonness |
| Vishesha | Particularity/differentiation |
| Samavaya | Inherence, an intimate inseparable relation in the relevant philosophical system |

These are separate concepts.

Similarly:

**Samanya ≠ Samavaya**

The similarity of their names can cause examination errors.

---

# 30. Samanya and the six Padarthas

In the classical Vaisheshika framework, the categories traditionally taught include:

1. Dravya
2. Guna
3. Karma
4. Samanya
5. Vishesha
6. Samavaya

Later philosophical developments discuss additional categories in some traditions.

For BAMS Padartha Vijnanam, the learner should understand Samanya as a distinct philosophical category and then understand how Ayurveda gives it practical therapeutic significance.

---

# 31. Contemporary science: useful analogies

NCISM asks learners to understand Samanya through principles and examples from contemporary sciences.

These should be used as **analogies**, not as proof that modern science and classical Ayurveda are identical.

## 31.1 Nutrition and tissue building

Modern nutrition recognizes that adequate supply of nutrients is necessary for growth, maintenance and repair.

This can help students visualize the Ayurvedic idea that appropriate nourishing inputs can support increase.

But:

**Samanya is a philosophical and Ayurvedic principle; nutrition science is not a literal translation of Samanya.**

## 31.2 Exercise adaptation

Repeated physical loading produces adaptation in tissues.

This offers an analogy for how repeated exposure to a relevant stimulus can increase a corresponding functional capacity.

## 31.3 Homeostatic regulation

Modern physiology emphasizes maintaining variables within functional ranges.

This helps students understand why “increase” should not be interpreted as unlimited accumulation.

## 31.4 Pharmacological dose-response

Dose, exposure and biological response are related.

This is a useful analogy for understanding why the effect of a similar factor depends on dose and context.

### Limitation

These comparisons are educational bridges, not evidence that contemporary science has experimentally validated every philosophical statement about Samanya.

---

# 32. Common misconceptions

### Misconception 1
**Samanya means only “general.”**

**Correction:** In Padartha Vijnanam it is a technical principle of commonness/generalization, and Ayurveda gives it functional therapeutic significance.

### Misconception 2
**Every similarity always increases everything.**

**Correction:** The relevant entity, quality, dose and clinical context must be considered.

### Misconception 3
**Samanya alone determines treatment.**

**Correction:** Diagnosis, Dosha, Dushya, Agni, Bala, Kala, Desha, dose and other factors must be assessed.

### Misconception 4
**Samanya and Samavaya are the same.**

**Correction:** They are distinct Padarthas.

### Misconception 5
**Samanya is useful only for medicines.**

**Correction:** It applies to Dravya, Guna, Karma, Ahara, Vihara and therapeutic procedures.

### Misconception 6
**Samanya always means beneficial increase.**

**Correction:** Increase can be desirable or pathological.

# 40. Integrated clinical case

### Case

A patient presents with reduced strength and poor nutritional status. Agni is assessed before initiating a nourishing plan.

### Reasoning

**1. Identify target:** nourishment and strength.

**2. Identify required direction:** Vriddhi.

**3. Apply Samanya:** choose appropriately nourishing factors with relevant qualities.

**4. Assess Agni:** avoid giving a quantity that exceeds digestive capacity.

**5. Individualize:** consider age, Prakriti, disease, Bala and tolerance.

**6. Monitor:** assess whether nourishment is occurring without producing unwanted Dosha aggravation.

### Conclusion

Samanya provides the direction of reasoning, but complete Ayurvedic management requires multiple clinical variables.

# 43. Summary

Samanya is a foundational concept in Padartha Vijnanam. Philosophically, it represents commonness or generality by which multiple particulars are understood as sharing a common character. In Vaisheshika it is treated as a distinct Padartha.

Ayurveda transforms this philosophical idea into a highly practical principle. Appropriate similarity is associated with increase, or Vriddhi, and this becomes an important basis for understanding Dosha aggravation, tissue nourishment, Ahara selection, Aushadha selection and therapeutic planning.

The principle must never be applied mechanically. Clinical outcome depends on the nature of the target, relevant qualities, Dravya, Karma, Agni, Bala, Prakriti, Kala, Desha, dose, duration and disease context.

The most important conceptual pair is:

**Samanya → increase**

**Vishesha → decrease**

Together they provide a central framework for Ayurvedic reasoning and therapeutic decision-making.
