# Chapter 16 — Karya–Karana Siddhanta

## NCISM First Professional BAMS — Padartha Vijnanam

### Chapter scope

**Karya–Karana Siddhanta (कार्य-कारण सिद्धान्त)** deals with the relationship between an effect (**Karya**) and its cause (**Karana**). It is a major bridge between philosophical reasoning and Ayurvedic understanding of disease, health, treatment and causation.

The official NCISM syllabus requires:
- Lakshana of Karya and Karana and types of Karana;
- significance of Karya and Karana in Ayurveda;
- major philosophical positions on manifestation of Karya from Karana;
- utility of these views in Ayurveda;
- cause-effect relationship, causality and causation in contemporary sciences.

NCISM also identifies analysis and application of Karya–Karana Bhava in Ayurveda as a course learning outcome.

---

# 1. Learning objectives

After studying this chapter, the learner should be able to:

- define Karya and Karana;
- explain Karya–Karana Bhava;
- describe major classifications of Karana;
- explain the significance of cause and effect in Ayurveda;
- understand the major philosophical theories of causation;
- distinguish Satkaryavada from Asatkaryavada;
- explain Parinamavada and Vivartavada;
- explain Arambhavada and Paramanuvada;
- understand Kshanabhanguravada;
- explain Pilupaka and Pitharapaka;
- understand Anekantavada, Swabhavavada and Swabhavoparamavada;
- relate these theories to Ayurvedic concepts without forcing false equivalence;
- analyse causation in diagnosis and therapeutics;
- distinguish cause, association, risk factor, trigger and coincidence;
- apply contemporary causal reasoning safely.

---

# 2. Meaning of Karya

**Karya (कार्य)** means the effect, product, consequence or manifested outcome.

In a cause-effect relation:

**Karana → Karya**

The Karya is that whose manifestation is understood in relation to one or more causes.

### Examples

- Ahara and lifestyle factors → change in health status
- Nidana → disease manifestation
- Appropriate treatment → therapeutic response
- Seed → plant
- Heat → change in temperature

These examples are useful for understanding the general idea, but philosophical theories differ about exactly **how** the effect exists in relation to the cause.

---

# 3. Meaning of Karana

**Karana (कारण)** means cause — that which is responsible for the production, manifestation or occurrence of an effect.

A cause should not be understood merely as:

> “Anything that happens before something else.”

Temporal priority alone does not establish causation.

---

# 4. Karya–Karana Bhava

**Karya–Karana Bhava** is the relation between an effect and its cause.

A basic conceptual sequence is:

**Karana → process/conditions → Karya**

In Ayurveda this relation is important because the physician constantly asks:

- What produced the disease?
- What maintains it?
- What aggravates it?
- What can remove or reduce it?
- What conditions are required for therapeutic effect?

---

# 5. Why causation matters in Ayurveda

Ayurvedic clinical reasoning is strongly concerned with causation.

Examples:

### Disease

**Nidana → Dosha disturbance → Dushya involvement → Samprapti → Vyadhi**

### Treatment

**Appropriate Chikitsa → correction of relevant pathological process → improvement**

### Prevention

**Avoidance of Nidana → reduction of causal exposure → prevention or reduction of disease risk**

### Health

**Proper Ahara + Vihara + appropriate routine → maintenance of equilibrium**

The exact causal chain varies with disease and individual context.

---

# 6. Lakshana of Karana

Different philosophical systems define Karana differently.

For undergraduate understanding, Karana is that which is appropriately related to the production or manifestation of Karya.

### Important caution

A cause is not necessarily:

- the only factor;
- the first factor in time;
- sufficient by itself;
- permanent;
- directly observable.

Many biological outcomes are **multifactorial**.

---

# 7. Types of Karana

Classifications differ among Darshanas.

A major Nyaya–Vaisheshika classification describes three kinds of causes:

1. **Samavayi Karana**
2. **Asamavayi Karana**
3. **Nimitta Karana**

---

# 8. Samavayi Karana

**Samavayi Karana** is the inhering/material cause, understood as a cause inseparably connected with the effect in the classical system.

### Classical type of example

The threads are understood as Samavayi Karana of cloth in the Nyaya–Vaisheshika analysis.

The material constituents are intimately related to the resulting product.

---

# 9. Asamavayi Karana

**Asamavayi Karana** is a cause that is not itself the direct material constituent in the same manner, but is related through the Samavayi cause and contributes to the production of the effect.

Classical examples involve qualities or relations associated with the material constituents.

### Learning point

The distinction is subtle and should be learned with the examples and definitions given in the prescribed Darshana text.

---

# 10. Nimitta Karana

**Nimitta Karana** is the instrumental or efficient cause.

Examples used in classical reasoning may include:

- artisan;
- tools;
- action;
- relevant external conditions.

### Simple conceptual example

For making a pot:

- clay provides the material basis;
- the potter and relevant activity function as instrumental factors.

---

# 11. Three-cause relationship

A simplified teaching diagram is:

**Samavayi cause**
↓  
materially connected basis

**Asamavayi cause**
↓  
associated causal factor

**Nimitta cause**
↓  
instrumental/efficient factor

**Karya**
↓  
manifested effect

The exact philosophical interpretation should be learned according to the relevant Darshana.

---

# 12. Karana in Ayurveda

Ayurveda uses causal reasoning in many contexts.

### Nidana

The cause or causative factor of disease.

### Dosha

A causal factor in disease development when aggravated and interacting with other factors.

### Dushya

Tissues or structures involved in manifestation.

### Samprapti

The sequence and interaction through which disease manifests.

### Chikitsa

An intervention intended to modify relevant causal processes.

---

# 13. Cause, condition and trigger

A useful clinical distinction:

### Cause

A factor that contributes causally to the outcome.

### Condition

A circumstance necessary or supportive for the causal process.

### Trigger

A factor that precipitates an event in a susceptible system.

### Risk factor

A factor associated with increased probability of an outcome.

### Correlation

A statistical association that does not by itself prove causation.

These distinctions are especially important when connecting classical causal reasoning with modern science.

# 14. Major theories of Karya–Karana

NCISM requires study of the following positions:

1. Satkaryavada
2. Parinamavada
3. Vivartavada
4. Asatkaryavada
5. Arambhavada
6. Paramanuvada
7. Kshanabhanguravada
8. Pilupaka
9. Pitharapaka
10. Anekantavada
11. Swabhavavada
12. Swabhavoparamavada

These theories should be understood comparatively rather than memorized as unrelated names.

---

# 15. Satkaryavada

**Satkaryavada** holds that the effect exists in some form in its cause before manifestation.

### Core idea

**Karya is pre-existent in Karana.**

The effect is manifested rather than being produced from absolute non-existence.

### Common teaching example

Curd is related to milk as an effect that emerges through transformation.

### Significance

The theory emphasizes continuity between cause and effect.

---

# 16. Ayurvedic significance of Satkaryavada

Ayurvedic processes often involve transformation rather than creation from absolute nothingness.

Examples for conceptual study:

- Ahara undergoing transformation;
- Dhatu nourishment and transformation;
- disease manifestation from accumulated causal factors;
- therapeutic change following modification of pathological processes.

### Caution

These examples illustrate causal continuity; they should not be presented as proof that every Ayurvedic process is philosophically identical to the classical Satkaryavada doctrine.

---

# 17. Parinamavada

**Parinamavada** explains the effect as a real transformation of the cause.

### Core idea

**Cause undergoes transformation → effect manifests.**

The transformation is regarded as real rather than merely apparent.

### Example

Milk undergoing transformation into curd is a standard teaching example.

---

# 18. Parinamavada and Ayurveda

Parinamavada is especially useful for understanding transformation-oriented processes.

Potential Ayurvedic applications include:

- digestion and metabolic transformation;
- Dhatu formation;
- maturation;
- pathological transformation;
- therapeutic transformation.

### Clinical principle

A physician should ask:

> “What process has changed the state of the system?”

This helps move from static description to causal understanding.

---

# 19. Vivartavada

**Vivartavada** explains the effect as an apparent transformation or manifestation in which the underlying reality is not transformed in the same way as the appearance suggests.

The classical philosophical context is especially associated with Advaita Vedanta.

### Famous teaching analogy

The **rope-snake** example:

- rope is present;
- snake is perceived due to error;
- the rope itself has not actually transformed into a snake.

### Core distinction

**Parinama = real transformation**

**Vivarta = apparent transformation**

---

# 20. Clinical relevance of Vivarta

Vivartavada should not be used to deny real disease.

Its educational value is in understanding:

- perception;
- interpretation;
- error;
- appearance versus underlying reality;
- distinction between observed phenomenon and its actual basis.

This can help students appreciate why clinical appearances must be interpreted carefully.

---

# 21. Asatkaryavada

**Asatkaryavada** holds that the effect does not pre-exist in the cause in the same manifested form.

The effect is regarded as newly produced.

### Core contrast

**Satkaryavada:** effect pre-exists in cause.

**Asatkaryavada:** effect is not pre-existent in the same sense.

This difference is central to Indian philosophical debates on causation.

---

# 22. Arambhavada

**Arambhavada** is associated with the Nyaya–Vaisheshika account of effect production.

The effect is understood as a new beginning or production arising from causal conditions.

### Key idea

The effect is not simply a pre-existing entity becoming manifest in the same way; it is newly produced through causal combination.

### Relation

Arambhavada is commonly discussed alongside Asatkaryavada.

---

# 23. Satkaryavada versus Asatkaryavada

| Feature | Satkaryavada | Asatkaryavada |
|---|---|---|
| Effect before manifestation | Exists in cause | Not pre-existent in same sense |
| Causal model | Manifestation/transformation | New production |
| Main emphasis | Continuity | Emergence |
| Typical philosophical association | Samkhya | Nyaya–Vaisheshika |
| Clinical teaching value | Transformation | Production through causal conditions |

---

# 24. Paramanuvada

**Paramanuvada** is associated with the atomistic account of Nyaya–Vaisheshika.

It explains gross objects through combinations of subtle/ultimate material units.

### Basic sequence

**Paramanu → combinations → larger units → gross object**

### Ayurvedic relevance

Paramanuvada can help students understand an Indian philosophical model of material composition.

However, classical Paramanu should not simply be equated with modern subatomic particles.

---

# 25. Paramanu and modern science

Modern physics studies:

- atoms;
- molecules;
- subatomic particles;
- fields;
- quantum states.

Classical Indian Paramanu is a philosophical concept embedded in a different ontology.

### Correct approach

**Compare concepts for learning; do not claim historical or scientific identity without evidence.**

---

# 26. Kshanabhanguravada

**Kshanabhanguravada** refers to the Buddhist momentariness doctrine.

The broad idea is:

**All conditioned phenomena are momentary and undergo continuous arising and cessation.**

### Causal significance

Causation is understood as a sequence of momentary events rather than a permanently enduring substance producing an effect.

---

# 27. Ayurvedic learning from Kshanabhanguravada

Its educational value includes understanding:

- change;
- succession;
- transient states;
- process rather than static entities.

It can encourage attention to dynamic disease processes.

### Caution

This does not mean that Ayurveda adopts the entire Buddhist doctrine.

---

# 28. Pilupaka

**Pilupaka** is associated with the Nyaya–Vaisheshika discussion of transformation and combination at the level of subtle units.

The doctrine is used in explaining how transformation in an object may be understood through changes occurring at the paramanu level.

### Teaching point

The theory provides one philosophical model for explaining material transformation.

---

# 29. Pitharapaka

**Pitharapaka** is another classical account of transformation.

It emphasizes transformation at the level of the already constituted gross object.

### Comparative memory

**Pilupaka → transformation explained through subtle units**

**Pitharapaka → transformation explained at the gross-object level**

The exact philosophical arguments should be studied from the prescribed Tarka/Nyaya source.

---

# 30. Pilupaka and Pitharapaka — comparison

| Feature | Pilupaka | Pitharapaka |
|---|---|---|
| Main explanatory level | Subtle/atomic units | Gross object |
| Focus | Transformation at constituent level | Transformation of constituted object |
| Context | Nyaya–Vaisheshika debate | Nyaya–Vaisheshika debate |
| Ayurvedic use | Conceptual model of transformation | Conceptual model of transformation |

---

# 31. Anekantavada

**Anekantavada** is associated with Jain philosophy.

It emphasizes the many-sided nature of reality.

An object or event may have multiple valid aspects depending upon the standpoint from which it is considered.

### Causal significance

A complex outcome may require consideration of multiple factors and perspectives.

---

# 32. Syadvada and causality

Anekantavada is commonly taught alongside the Jain doctrine of conditional predication, **Syadvada**.

The important educational lesson is:

- avoid simplistic one-sided conclusions;
- consider context;
- distinguish different standpoints;
- qualify claims appropriately.

### Clinical analogy

A disease may have:

- multiple causes;
- multiple manifestations;
- multiple contributing factors;
- different interpretations at different levels.

This is an analogy, not a claim that clinical medicine is identical to Jain epistemology.

---

# 33. Swabhavavada

**Swabhavavada** explains phenomena through their inherent nature or intrinsic properties.

The idea gives importance to the natural characteristics of things.

### Ayurvedic relevance

Ayurveda repeatedly considers the inherent properties of:

- Dravya;
- Guna;
- Rasa;
- Virya;
- Vipaka;
- Dosha;
- Dhatu;
- Mala.

However, the philosophical doctrine of Swabhavavada should not be equated wholesale with Ayurvedic Dravya-Guna theory.

---

# 34. Swabhavoparamavada

**Swabhavoparamavada** is particularly relevant to Ayurvedic thought concerning cessation of processes.

A useful teaching formulation is:

**A process ceases when its causative conditions cease or when the natural course of cessation operates.**

This is connected with the Ayurvedic discussion of disease and the ending of pathological processes.

### Clinical importance

The physician should consider both:

- causes of disease production;
- causes or conditions of disease cessation.

---

# 35. Production and cessation

A simple Ayurvedic causal model is:

**Nidana present**

↓

**Pathological process continues**

↓

**Disease manifestation**

If relevant Nidana are removed:

**Nidana removal**

↓

**causal chain is interrupted**

↓

**pathological process reduces**

↓

**disease subsides**

This illustrates why **Nidana Parivarjana** is foundational in treatment.

---

# 36. Swabhavoparama and treatment

Treatment does not always “create health” in a simplistic sense.

It may:

- remove causes;
- reduce aggravating factors;
- restore supportive conditions;
- assist natural recovery;
- prevent further injury;
- restore functional equilibrium.

Thus therapeutic reasoning involves both **causal production** and **causal cessation**.

# 38. Karya–Karana in Nidana

A clinician asks:

### 1. What is the Karya?

The disease or pathological outcome.

### 2. What are the Karana?

Relevant Nidana and contributing factors.

### 3. What is the causal sequence?

The Samprapti.

### 4. Which causal links can be interrupted?

Targets of prevention and treatment.

### 5. What should happen when the causal factor is removed?

Reduction or cessation of the pathological process, depending on the disease and patient.

---

# 39. Karya–Karana in Chikitsa

Treatment can be analysed through causal reasoning.

### Example framework

**Nidana**

↓

**Dosha/Dushya interaction**

↓

**Samprapti**

↓

**Vyadhi**

↓

**Chikitsa**

↓

**Causal modification**

↓

**Improvement**

This is not a universal linear model; actual disease processes may be reciprocal and multifactorial.

---

# 40. Samprapti and causation

**Samprapti** is especially valuable for understanding causal sequences in Ayurveda.

It helps identify:

- causative factors;
- Dosha involvement;
- Dushya involvement;
- Srotas;
- site of manifestation;
- stages of progression;
- factors maintaining disease.

### Clinical value

Understanding Samprapti helps the physician select the appropriate therapeutic target.

---

# 41. Nidana Parivarjana

**Nidana Parivarjana** means avoidance/removal of causative factors.

It demonstrates practical application of Karya–Karana reasoning.

### Sequence

**Nidana**

→ disease process

If Nidana is removed:

**causal input decreases**

→ disease process may reduce

This is why identifying causative factors is not merely theoretical.

---

# 42. Multifactorial causation

Modern biology frequently demonstrates that a single outcome can depend upon many factors.

For example:

**Disease outcome**

= genetic susceptibility  
+ environmental exposure  
+ nutrition  
+ behaviour  
+ infection  
+ age  
+ comorbidities  
+ social determinants  
+ chance/random variation

This can be compared pedagogically with Ayurvedic multifactorial causal reasoning.

### Important

The two frameworks should not be declared identical.

---

# 43. Necessary, sufficient and contributory causes

Modern causal reasoning distinguishes:

### Necessary cause

A factor that must be present for a particular outcome under the defined conditions.

### Sufficient cause

A causal condition or combination that is enough to produce the outcome under defined conditions.

### Contributory cause

A factor that increases or contributes to the probability of an outcome but may not be sufficient by itself.

This vocabulary helps students avoid oversimplified causal claims.

---

# 44. Correlation is not causation

If two variables change together, that does not establish that one caused the other.

Possible explanations include:

- direct causation;
- reverse causation;
- confounding;
- selection bias;
- coincidence.

### Ayurvedic clinical lesson

A temporal association between a food, behaviour or exposure and a symptom should be interpreted using the full clinical context rather than automatically declared causal.

---

# 45. Charakokta Dashvidha Parikshya Bhava

**Dashvidha Parikshya Bhava** is Charaka's tenfold set of factors to be examined before initiating an action. It is explicitly included in the NCISM learning objectives under Karya–Karana Siddhanta and should be studied as an applied form of causal and purposeful planning.

The ten factors are:

| Factor | Core meaning / application |
|---|---|
| **Karana** | The doer, chief agent or cause/reason relevant to accomplishment of the action |
| **Karana (instrument)** | The instrument or means used for accomplishing the action |
| **Karyayoni** | Source/origin from which the action or effect arises |
| **Karya** | The action/effect to be accomplished |
| **Karyaphala** | The fruit or outcome expected from the action |
| **Anubandha** | Subsequent consequence or continuing result |
| **Desha** | Place, habitat or setting in which the action occurs |
| **Kala** | Time/season or temporal circumstance |
| **Pravritti** | Initiation or undertaking of the action |
| **Upaya** | The method, strategy or means adopted to accomplish it |

A published review of Charaka's formulation describes these ten factors as a structured method of investigation that supports proper planning and helps avoid aimless intellectual activity.

### Clinical interpretation

Before treatment, the physician should examine:

- **Karana** — the responsible agent and causal/reasoning basis;
- **instrument** — appropriate therapeutic tools, including Bheshaja;
- **Karyayoni** — the source or condition from which the therapeutic problem/action arises;
- **Karya** — the desired therapeutic action or state;
- **Karyaphala** — expected clinical outcome;
- **Anubandha** — subsequent or longer-term consequences;
- **Desha** — patient and environmental setting;
- **Kala** — appropriate time/season;
- **Pravritti** — whether and how treatment is initiated;
- **Upaya** — the practical strategy used.

### Research application

The same framework can structure an Ayurvedic research plan:

**Researcher → methods/instruments → research problem → objectives → expected outcomes → longer-term implications → study setting → time → implementation → strategy**

This is a useful modern application, but the classical concept should not be declared identical to any single modern research-design framework.

### Relation to Karya–Karana

Dashvidha Parikshya Bhava extends causal reasoning beyond simply asking **“What is the cause?”** It asks the learner to examine the **agent, instruments, origin, action, outcome, consequences, place, time, initiation and means** before acting.

It is therefore useful for:

- clinical decision-making;
- treatment planning;
- prevention;
- research planning;
- structured problem solving.

### Exam-ready answer

**Charakokta Dashvidha Parikshya Bhava are ten factors to be examined before undertaking an action: Karana, Karana (instrument), Karyayoni, Karya, Karyaphala, Anubandha, Desha, Kala, Pravritti and Upaya. They provide a structured method for planning action and understanding cause-effect relationships in clinical and other organized activities.**

# 47. Contemporary causal reasoning

Modern causal science asks questions such as:

- What would happen if the exposure were removed?
- What happens under intervention?
- Is the association reproducible?
- Are alternative explanations controlled?
- Is there biological plausibility?
- Is the temporal sequence appropriate?
- Is there a dose-response pattern?
- Does experimental or quasi-experimental evidence support causality?

These questions enrich causal reasoning but should not be retroactively projected onto every classical doctrine.

---

# 47. Causation versus association

| Feature | Association | Causation |
|---|---|---|
| Variables related | Yes | Yes |
| One contributes to producing another | Not established | Supported |
| Confounding possible | Yes | Must be addressed |
| Intervention evidence | Not necessary | Often highly informative |
| Clinical implication | Cautious | Potentially actionable |

A useful working model:

**Exposure/Nidana**

↓

**Biological/functional response**

↓

**Intermediate changes**

↓

**Disease manifestation**

↓

**Clinical outcome**

↓

↓

# 49. Contemporary example — infection

A simplified modern model:

**Pathogen exposure**

→ infection under susceptible conditions

→ host response

→ pathological process

→ symptoms

→ clinical disease

Not every exposed person necessarily develops the same outcome.

Factors include:

- immunity;
- dose;
- route;
- host condition;
- coexisting illness;
- environment.

### Learning point

Causation can be conditional and multifactorial.

---

# 50. Contemporary example — lifestyle disease

Consider metabolic disease.

Potential contributors include:

- diet;
- physical activity;
- sleep;
- genetics;
- age;
- medications;
- endocrine factors;
- social and environmental conditions.

No single factor necessarily explains every case.

This illustrates why causal reasoning must be patient-specific.

---

# 51. Contemporary example — therapeutic intervention

Suppose a treatment is associated with improvement.

A researcher should ask:

- Was the improvement caused by treatment?
- Did the disease naturally fluctuate?
- Was there placebo/context effect?
- Were other treatments changed?
- Was there regression to the mean?
- Was the outcome measured objectively?
- Was the study adequately controlled?

This is modern causal analysis.

---

# 52. Karya–Karana and research design

A research question should identify:

**Cause/exposure/intervention → outcome**

Example:

> Does intervention X reduce outcome Y in patients with condition Z?

A strong study then defines:

- population;
- intervention;
- comparator;
- outcome;
- time;
- confounders;
- analysis plan.

### Ayurvedic research

This framework can be applied to:

- Aushadha;
- Panchakarma;
- Ahara interventions;
- lifestyle interventions;
- preventive strategies;
- Rasayana research.

---

# 53. Karya–Karana and evidence

A causal claim becomes stronger when multiple lines of evidence converge.

Potential evidence includes:

- temporal relationship;
- reproducibility;
- dose-response;
- mechanistic plausibility;
- experimental evidence;
- observational evidence;
- dechallenge/rechallenge where ethically appropriate;
- consistency across studies.

No single criterion should be treated as a universal proof of causation in every setting.

---

# 54. Causal reasoning in therapeutics

Before prescribing, ask:

1. What is the primary problem?
2. What are the relevant causes?
3. Which factors maintain the problem?
4. Which causes are modifiable?
5. What intervention targets them?
6. What adverse effects can the intervention create?
7. How will response be measured?
8. What alternative explanations exist?

This converts Karya–Karana from abstract philosophy into clinical reasoning.

---

# 55. Clinical case 1 — Nidana Parivarjana

### Case

A patient repeatedly develops symptoms after a particular dietary and behavioural pattern.

### Causal analysis

- identify temporal relationship;
- assess reproducibility;
- evaluate other factors;
- determine whether the exposure is plausibly causal;
- remove or modify the suspected Nidana where appropriate;
- monitor outcome.

### Learning point

**Causal reasoning guides prevention as well as treatment.**

---

# 56. Clinical case 2 — Multifactorial disease

### Case

A patient presents with a chronic condition and has multiple contributing factors.

### Incorrect approach

> “One factor is present, therefore it is the only cause.”

### Better approach

Construct a causal map:

**Predisposition**

+ **exposures**

+ **behaviour**

+ **diet**

+ **environment**

+ **disease-specific factors**

↓

**clinical outcome**

This is more appropriate for complex disorders.

---

# 57. Clinical case 3 — Therapeutic response

A patient improves after receiving a treatment.

Does improvement prove that the treatment alone caused recovery?

### Answer

No.

Possible contributors include:

- natural course;
- concurrent treatment;
- behavioural changes;
- placebo/contextual effects;
- regression to the mean;
- measurement variation.

Causal attribution requires appropriate evaluation.

# 58. Common misconceptions

### Misconception 1
**Every preceding event is a cause.**

**Correction:** Temporal sequence alone does not establish causation.

### Misconception 2
**A disease has only one cause.**

**Correction:** Many diseases are multifactorial.

### Misconception 3
**Satkaryavada and Parinamavada are identical terms.**

**Correction:** They are closely related in certain philosophical systems but emphasize different aspects: pre-existence of effect versus real transformation.

### Misconception 4
**Vivartavada means disease is unreal.**

**Correction:** It is a philosophical account of apparent transformation; it should not be casually applied to deny clinical disease.

### Misconception 5
**Paramanu is the same as the modern atom.**

**Correction:** Classical Paramanu is a philosophical concept and should not be equated automatically with modern physics.

### Misconception 6
**Anekantavada means all opinions are equally true.**

**Correction:** Its philosophical meaning is more precise and concerns the many-sided nature of reality and conditional standpoints.

### Misconception 7
**Swabhavoparamavada means treatment is unnecessary.**

**Correction:** Understanding cessation of causal processes does not eliminate the therapeutic role of removing causes and supporting recovery.

### Misconception 8
**Correlation proves causation.**

**Correction:** Association requires causal evaluation.

# 60. Exam-ready definitions

### Karana

### Karya–Karana Bhava

**Karya–Karana Bhava is the causal relation between an effect and its cause.**

### Satkaryavada

**Satkaryavada is the doctrine that the effect exists in some form in its cause before manifestation.**

### Parinamavada

**Parinamavada explains the effect as a real transformation of the cause.**

### Vivartavada

**Vivartavada explains the effect as an apparent transformation without corresponding real transformation of the underlying basis.**

### Asatkaryavada

**Asatkaryavada holds that the effect is not pre-existent in the cause in the same sense and is produced as a new effect.**

# 71. Summary

Karya–Karana Siddhanta is the systematic study of effect and cause. It is essential to understanding how events, processes and outcomes are related.

The chapter begins with Karya and Karana and the classical classification of causes. It then examines major Indian philosophical explanations of how an effect arises from a cause: Satkaryavada, Parinamavada, Vivartavada, Asatkaryavada, Arambhavada, Paramanuvada, Kshanabhanguravada, Pilupaka, Pitharapaka, Anekantavada, Swabhavavada and Swabhavoparamavada.

In Ayurveda, causal reasoning is directly relevant to Nidana, Samprapti, prevention and Chikitsa. **Nidana Parivarjana** is a particularly clear practical expression of the principle: identify relevant causes and remove or reduce them where possible.

Contemporary science adds formal approaches to causality, including intervention, confounding, multifactorial models and causal inference. These are valuable for modern Ayurvedic research, but classical philosophical doctrines and modern causal science should be compared carefully rather than declared identical.

### Central principle

**Understand the cause → understand the effect → identify the causal chain → modify the appropriate causal factor → assess the resulting effect.**
